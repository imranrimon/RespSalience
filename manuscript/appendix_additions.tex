\section{Full Multi-Metric Comparison}
\label{app:fullmetric}
%% source: scripts/full_metric_comparison.py --deepphys100
Table~\ref{tab:fullmetric} reports all ten evaluation metrics for PRISM-Breath and the five published
extractors on the 298 clips common to every method, with subject-grouped bootstrap 95\% confidence
intervals ($2{,}000$ resamples). Metrics span three families: threshold-free discrimination of tachypnea
(AUROC on the calibrated probability, AUROC ranked by the raw rate--threshold margin, AUROC ranked by the
raw predicted rate, and AUPRC), screening operating points (sensitivity at $90\%$ specificity, specificity
at $90\%$ sensitivity, and the WHO-decision sensitivity/specificity), and regression error (MAE, RMSE).
PRISM-Breath holds the best point estimate on $6$ of $10$ metrics---every discrimination metric plus the
screening-critical specificity-at-$90\%$-sensitivity (where the extractors collapse to $0.05$--$0.21$) and
RMSE. The four metrics the baselines win are sensitivity and Se@Sp$90$ (DeepPhys), raw specificity
(PhysFormer, which achieves it by under-alarming: its sensitivity is only $0.47$), and MAE (DeepPhys,
$5.62$ vs.\ PRISM $6.42$). This dissociation is the paper's thesis in one table: the model with the lowest
regression error is not the best-\emph{ranking} tachypnea screen, because a $\sim$$0.8$~breath/min MAE
advantage does not translate into threshold-free discrimination. The table is also where the limits of our
model are most visible, and we present it in full rather than selectively: DeepPhys is better on AUPRC, on
sensitivity at the WHO cutpoint, and on Se@Sp90.

Table~\ref{tab:paired} gives the paired significance tests against PRISM-Breath: the fast-DeLong test on
the AUROC difference and the exact McNemar test on the discordant WHO decisions. The two tests point in
different directions, and we state both plainly because the disagreement is itself the paper's point.
PRISM-Breath ranks better than every baseline, significantly so against three of the five (TS-CAN and
PhysFormer $p=0.007$, EfficientPhys $p=0.040$; VIRENet $p=0.055$ and DeepPhys $p=0.137$ do not reach
significance). But at the \emph{default} WHO cutpoint applied to the predicted rate, PRISM is
significantly \emph{worse} than two of them (DeepPhys $p=0.008$, VIRENet $p=0.028$), because its
fixed-threshold sensitivity is lower ($0.543$ vs.\ $0.716$ and $0.691$).

We do not regard this as a contradiction, and it should not be read as one. It is the read-out failure
this paper is about, measured on our own model: a rate regressor shrinks its extreme predictions toward
the mean, so thresholding the point estimate under-detects even where the underlying ranking is better.
The remedy we advocate is not a better regressor but a different read-out---an age-conditional operating
point on the calibrated distribution, which raises neonatal sensitivity from $0.10$ to $0.70$
(Section~\ref{sec:age})---and a sensitivity-first operating point, where the advantage reappears
decisively (Sp@Se90 $0.346$ vs.\ $0.046$--$0.207$). A reader who intends to deploy the naive WHO cutpoint
on a predicted rate should prefer DeepPhys to our model; that is the honest reading of
Table~\ref{tab:paired}, and it is exactly the practice this paper argues against.

\section{Subgroup and Fairness Analysis}
\label{app:subgroup}
%% source: scripts/subgroup_fairness.py --deepphys100
Tables~\ref{tab:agesubgroup}--\ref{tab:sitesubgroup} stratify tachypnea AUROC by clinical age band and by
recording site on the common 298-clip set. Three patterns emerge, and the third is unfavourable to our
model. First, PRISM-Breath's advantage concentrates in the \emph{hard} regimes: it leads in the
$2$--$12$-month band ($0.860$ vs.\ $0.73$--$0.80$) and at the two harder, higher-prevalence sites (ICMH,
$0.757$ vs.\ $0.64$--$0.71$; Upazila HC, $0.903$ vs.\ $0.79$--$0.87$). Second, the $<2$-month band remains
the hardest: PRISM's discrimination is weak (AUROC $0.676$ on the matched $298$-set, $0.650$ on the full
$332$; $n{=}35$/$38$, very wide CI) and its WHO-threshold sensitivity collapses to $0.10$, because the
saturated camera rate ($\sim$$30$) rarely reaches the neonatal $\ge 60$~breath/min cutoff; the CNN
extractors are at or below chance ($0.20$--$0.36$), though PhysFormer matches PRISM here ($0.680$). This
band motivates the escalation pathway of Section~\ref{app:escalation} rather than a screening claim.

Third, in the \emph{easy} regimes PRISM is not merely tied---it is last. In the $12$--$60$-month band it
scores $0.919$ against $0.925$--$0.947$ for the five extractors, and at the low-prevalence Community
Clinic VIRENet reaches $0.996$ against PRISM's $0.921$. The confidence intervals overlap heavily and we
do not claim a real deficit, but neither will we describe this as PRISM's ``edge being null.'' The
pattern across all three cuts is consistent and worth stating plainly: the decision-aware model buys
discrimination where rates are high and estimates are unreliable, and buys nothing---possibly a little
less than nothing---where the regression problem is already easy. That is what a read-out-level
intervention should be expected to do, and it is a reason to deploy it selectively by age band rather
than uniformly.

Table~\ref{tab:conformal} contrasts the two conformalizations at $\alpha{=}0.20$ (target coverage $0.80$),
both calibrated leave-one-fold-out so that no calibration subject appears in the fold being scored. A
single model-level width is \emph{exactly right on average and wrong in every band}: aggregate coverage
is $0.80$, but the same $9.5$~breath/min interval under-covers neonates at $0.58$ and over-covers the easy
$12$--$60$-month band at $0.87$. Aggregate coverage therefore certifies nothing about the band a screen
most needs to be honest in. Replacing the single width with a per-band $\hat q$ (Mondrian) restores
$0.81$--$0.82$ everywhere, at the cost of admitting how wide the neonatal interval really is
($\pm19$ vs.\ $\pm7$ breath/min for $12$--$60$mo)---an interval so wide it amounts to a deferral, which is
the honest read-out of that band and is what the escalation pathway (Section~\ref{app:escalation}) acts on.

Table~\ref{tab:riskcov} compares two abstention signals on the risk--coverage curve. Ranking cases by
probability confidence lowers the tachypnea error from $0.161$ at full coverage to $0.061$ when the most
uncertain $40\%$ are deferred (AURC $0.069$); the cross-modal \emph{disagreement} score, by contrast, is a
poor abstention signal (error \emph{rises} to $0.246$; AURC $0.240$). The operational triage that keys on
disagreement therefore auto-clears $62\%$ of cases at accuracy $0.902$ but at a reduced sensitivity of
$0.585$, while the $38\%$ it refers carry sensitivity $0.732$---i.e., disagreement routes many true
positives to referral rather than resolving them, which is why probability-confidence is the preferred
deferral rule.

\label{app:framing}

The main paper argues that classifying the waveform directly beats regressing a rate and thresholding it.
Two controls here show the effect is not an artifact of the specific WHO tachypnea cutoff. First,
Table~\ref{tab:brady} flips the direction of clinical interest to \emph{bradypnea} ($\text{GT}\le

for every backbone, with the gap significant by DeLong ($+0.028$ to $+0.072$ AUROC). The classification
framing therefore helps at both tails, not only for high rates.

\section{Neonatal Escalation Pathway}
\label{app:escalation}
%% source: scripts/escalation_pathway.py
Because every camera extractor collapses in the $<2$-month band (Section~\ref{app:subgroup}), the deployed
system does not screen neonates---it \emph{escalates} them. A leakage-free leave-one-fold-out
age-conditional (Mondrian) conformal interval decides, per case, whether the WHO threshold is unambiguously
outside the interval (auto-decide NORMAL/ALERT) or straddles it (REFER to a contact/radar modality).
Table~\ref{tab:escalation} shows the referral rate rises steeply with clinical risk: $74\%$ of $<2$-month
infants are escalated versus $39\%$ of the $12$--$60$-month band. On the cases the system \emph{retains}
(Table~\ref{tab:retained}) it screens at high sensitivity in the older bands ($1.000$ for $12$--$60$mo,
$0.636$ for $2$--$12$mo) and pooled sensitivity $0.808$/specificity $0.986$. Concretely, of the $10$
tachypneic $<2$-month neonates that a naive camera-only screen would almost entirely clear as normal
($\text{Sens}=0.100$), escalation routes $9$ to referral, cutting the missed-neonate count from $9$ to $1$.
The coverage cost is explicit and we state it rather than only the benefit: the system screens $52\%$ of
the cohort and escalates $48\%$, which is what buys the rise in retained sensitivity from $0.548$ to
$0.808$. Note also that the single retained tachypneic neonate is still missed, so the residual neonatal
risk is reduced by escalation, not eliminated.

\noindent\textbf{Honest caveats.} The rescue is real but bounded. (i) It requires the \emph{age-conditional}
$\hat q$ (per-band width $\sim$$26$~breath/min for $<2$mo vs.\ $\sim$$6.5$ for $12$--$60$mo); the persisted
single model-level width ($\sim$$8$~breath/min) escalates only $10\%$ of neonates and rescues $0/8$. (ii)
Escalation buys sensitivity with coverage: overall retained coverage is $56\%$ ($44\%$ of cases are
referred rather than screened), which lifts retained sensitivity from the full-coverage $0.663$ to $0.735$.
(iii) Even with age-conditional escalation, $2$ of the $8$ tachypneic neonates are still auto-cleared, so
the pathway mitigates but does not eliminate the neonatal blind spot. A decision-focused end-to-end model
that learns the referral policy directly is the natural next step.
\subsection{Decision-Focused Training Does Not Improve on Calibration Alone}
\label{app:decisionmodel}
%% source: scripts/decision_model_eval.py
The preceding analysis flagged an end-to-end model that learns the referral policy directly as the natural
next step. We built exactly that: PRISM retrained with a differentiable net-benefit (decision-curve)
objective---a soft decision $\sigma(20(p-p_t))$ integrated over the screening threshold grid
$p_t\in[0.05,0.30]$, plus a calibrated abstention gate---under the identical five-fold protocol.
\emph{The decision-focused variant does not improve clinical decision value; if anything it is slightly
worse.} On the common $297$-clip set it lowers AUROC ($0.840$ vs.\ $0.869$) and, by trading sensitivity for
specificity ($0.549/0.949$ vs.\ $0.659/0.907$), reduces net benefit at every operating point in the
screening range (Table~\ref{tab:decmodel}). The paired net-benefit difference at the central threshold
$p_t{=}0.15$ is $-0.019$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.040,+0.002]$; $P(\text{decision}{>}\text{standard})=0.034$), i.e.\
standard PRISM is favored. The reading is instructive rather than disappointing: once the estimate is
\emph{framed as classification and calibrated} (ECE $0.064$), the model already sits near the
decision-optimal frontier, so an explicit decision-curve loss finds no residual signal and merely shifts the
operating point toward specificity---the wrong direction for a sensitivity-first screen. It is calibration,
not a bespoke decision loss, that delivers the decision-curve advantage.

\section{Framing Mechanism: Boundary Divergence}
\label{app:mechanism}
%% source: scripts/framing_phase_diagram.py
Why should classifying a waveform and regressing-then-thresholding it ever disagree, given identical inputs?
This section reports a \emph{negative} control that bounds the claim, and we give it in full because it
constrains how the framing result should be read.

The cheapest possible way to ``reframe'' a rate regressor is to bolt a probability head onto it and train
both jointly, which is what PRISM-Breath's tachypnea head is. Comparing that head against thresholding the
same model's own rate isolates exactly this manoeuvre. It buys almost nothing: on all $332$ subjects the
gain is $+0.004$ AUROC ($0.827$ vs.\ $0.823$; bootstrap $[{-}0.014,{+}0.025]$, $P(\text{gain}{>}0)=0.65$),
the two framings return the \emph{same} binary decision on $317/332$ cases, and on the $15$ that flip the
classification head is correct only $46.7\%$ of the time---no better than a coin toss. Stratifying by
noise or by distance-to-cutoff reveals no consistent structure (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}); the tertile
gains alternate in sign.

The flips do sit at the boundary, as expected: their mean distance from the cutoff is $2.8$~breath/min
against $11.1$ for the agreeing cases, and their mean cross-modal disagreement is higher ($0.72$ vs.\
$0.49$). So the \emph{locus} of divergence is where the mechanism predicts. What is absent is any
\emph{benefit} from divergence.

We draw the conclusion this supports rather than the one we had hoped for. The framing gain reported in
the main paper (Section~\ref{sec:framing}) is not obtainable by attaching a classification head to a
regressor trained primarily to regress; it requires actually training the decision on the signal---a
ROCKET classifier on the waveform, or a salience network over rates, which is how those experiments were
run and where the gains of $+0.075$ to $+0.200$ appear. This is the same lesson as the component
isolation, which found that a deep head added nothing over a logistic regression on the encoder outputs.
An auxiliary probability head is not a reframing; it is a calibration layer.

\section{Multiple-comparison control and deployment operating characteristics}\label{sec:sdeploy}
\textbf{Family-wise error control.} The five head-to-head AUROC comparisons against PRISM form a test
family. Controlling the false-discovery rate with the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure, \emph{two} of the
five survive at $q<0.05$ (TS-CAN and PhysFormer, both $q=0.018$); EfficientPhys ($q=0.067$), VIRENet
($q=0.069$) and DeepPhys ($q=0.137$) do not (Table~\ref{tab:fdr}). We report the corrected values rather
than the uncorrected ones. The ranking advantage over the weakest two extractors is robust to multiple
testing; the advantage over the strongest two is not established at this sample size, and we describe it
as a consistent direction rather than a demonstrated effect.

\textbf{Deployment PPV/NPV across prevalence.} Predictive values depend on base rate, not the $27.5\%$
cohort prevalence. At a sensitivity-first operating point (Se${\ge}0.90$, Sp${=}0.644$), NPV stays very high
