\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Goal:} Non-contact respiratory-rate (RR) estimation from cameras is maturing, but its
translation to the clinical target that matters for infant acute respiratory infection---\emph{tachypnea}
screening by World Health Organization (WHO) age band---is weak, and the field optimizes RR
mean-absolute-error (MAE) rather than clinical discrimination. \textbf{Methods:} On a 332-subject
pediatric benchmark evaluated with subject-disjoint five-fold cross-validation, subject-level bootstrap
confidence intervals, and paired DeLong/McNemar tests, we compare four published extractors against
PRISM-Breath, a reliability-weighted multimodal (depth, optical-flow, audio) model with self-supervised
pretraining, calibrated tachypnea head, and age-conditional conformal triage. \textbf{Results:} The four
extractors match or beat PRISM on RR-MAE yet are all significantly worse on tachypnea area-under-ROC
(AUROC $0.869$ vs.\ $0.727$--$0.752$; DeLong $p\le0.0016$)---raw MAE is not clinical utility. An
ablation localizes the advantage to self-supervised pretraining and spectral/temporal encoding; a
\emph{lean} model that drops a prototype bank, a codebook, and a heteroscedastic head performs
identically. A component isolation instead localizes the performance to the per-modality \emph{encoder}: the raw
extracted flow discriminates tachypnea at only AUROC $0.72$ (FFT), a self-supervised spectral-temporal
encoder lifts this to $0.87$ ($p<10^{-4}$), and the deep fusion head adds nothing ($0.88$; a logistic
regression on the encoder outputs matches it). Going further, reframing the task as \emph{classification} of the
signal rather than rate regression is the largest single gain---AUROC $0.92$--$0.93$, robust across a
purpose-built salience network, its self-supervised variant, and a generic random-kernel classifier---and
it recovers neonatal discrimination that regression loses ($0.62$ to $0.88$). RGB optical flow
physically saturates near $35$~breaths/min for true neonatal rates of
$60$--$80$, so every camera extractor misses neonatal tachypnea; we therefore report a calibrated screen
for infants $\ge$2~months (sensitivity $0.71$, AUROC $0.919$, expected calibration error $0.064$,
positive net benefit) with mandatory neonatal referral. Radar recovers the neonatal band that RGB
cannot---MAE $0.00$ on a thorax phantom and $1.76$ ($r{=}0.96$) on $50$ real children.
\textbf{Conclusion/Significance:} We argue the field should report the clinical endpoint and be explicit
about age; PRISM-Breath is an honest, ablation-justified, calibrated screen, and radar charts a
sensing-modality route to the neonate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{P}{neumonia} is a leading infectious cause of death in children under
five~\cite{who_pneumonia}, and the WHO Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI)
algorithm~\cite{who_imci} screens for it using \emph{tachypnea}: fast breathing above age-specific
thresholds ($\ge$60~breaths/min for $<$2~months, $\ge$50 for 2--12~months, $\ge$40 for 12--60~months).
Manual breath counting over a full minute is the field reference but is labor-intensive and error-prone,
motivating automated, contactless alternatives that a frontline health worker could run from a phone.

Camera-based RR estimation is an attractive candidate. A large literature---classical
chrominance/plane-orthogonal methods~\cite{chrom_dehaan,pos_wang}, Eulerian motion
magnification~\cite{evm_wu}, and deep remote-photoplethysmography (rPPG) networks such as
PhysNet, DeepPhys, TS-CAN, and EfficientPhys~\cite{yu_physnet,mcduff_deepphys,liu_mttscan,liu_efficientphys}
consolidated in the rPPG-Toolbox~\cite{liu_rppgtoolbox}, together with infant-specific optical-flow
methods~\cite{infant_air}---reports RR-MAEs of only a few breaths/min. Taken at face value, the problem
looks solved.

We argue it is not, for two reasons that this paper makes concrete. \emph{First, the field optimizes the
wrong quantity.} Minimizing RR-MAE and maximizing tachypnea \emph{discrimination} are different
objectives: a regressor can have low average error yet rank tachypneic and normal infants poorly near
the decision threshold. We show, under matched cross-validation, that four state-of-the-art extractors
match or beat our model on MAE while being significantly worse on the clinical endpoint.
\emph{Second, the lowest-risk group is physically unreachable by RGB.} For true neonatal rates of
$60$--$80$~breaths/min, the extracted optical-flow waveform saturates near $35$; no camera method we
tested recovers neonatal tachypnea. Rather than hide this behind a favorable average, we make it a
design decision (defer neonates to a clinician) and show that a different sensing modality---radar---%
recovers the band that RGB cannot.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{metric critique with evidence}: on 298 matched subjects, four published extractors lose to
PRISM-Breath on tachypnea AUROC ($0.727$--$0.752$ vs.\ $0.869$; DeLong $p\le0.0016$) despite comparable
or better RR-MAE (Section~\ref{sec:sota}).
\item \emph{A component isolation of where the signal comes from}: the raw extracted flow discriminates
tachypnea at only AUROC $0.72$, a self-supervised spectral-temporal \emph{encoder} lifts it to $0.87$
($p<10^{-4}$), and the deep fusion head adds nothing ($0.88$; a logistic regression on the encoder outputs
matches it). The learned encoder---not the extraction, not the fusion architecture---is the load-bearing
component (Sections~\ref{sec:abl},~\ref{sec:simple}).
\item \emph{A reframing that is the largest gain}: casting tachypnea as signal \emph{classification}
rather than rate regression raises AUROC to $0.92$--$0.93$ (from $0.879$), robustly across three unrelated
classifiers, and recovers neonatal discrimination regression loses; we give a calibrated, ceiling-free
salience realization (RespSalience, Sec.~\ref{sec:framing}).
\item \emph{An honest, calibrated $\ge$2mo screen}: sensitivity $0.71$, AUROC $0.919$, expected
calibration error $0.064$, and positive net benefit on decision-curve analysis, with a documented
neonatal-referral policy (Sections~\ref{sec:age},~\ref{sec:calib}).
\item \emph{A preliminary radar path to the neonate}: on a phantom (MAE $0.00$) and $50$ real children
(MAE $1.76$, $r{=}0.96$), radar recovers the neonatal band RGB cannot see---a motivating result on
\emph{separate} cohorts, not yet an integrated device (Section~\ref{sec:radar}).
\end{enumerate}
Throughout, we use subject-disjoint five-fold cross-validation, subject-level bootstrap $95\%$ confidence
intervals~\cite{efron_bootstrap}, and paired DeLong~\cite{delong} and McNemar tests, and we report the
McNemar result honestly even where it tempers our AUROC claim.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Camera-based rPPG and respiratory rate}
Early contactless vitals used skin-color chrominance (CHROM~\cite{chrom_dehaan}), plane-orthogonal-to-skin
(POS~\cite{pos_wang}), or Eulerian magnification of subtle motion~\cite{evm_wu}. Deep networks then
learned the mapping end-to-end: DeepPhys~\cite{mcduff_deepphys} with an attention branch,
TS-CAN/MTTS-CAN~\cite{liu_mttscan} with temporal-shift modules, PhysNet~\cite{yu_physnet} with
spatio-temporal convolutions, and EfficientPhys~\cite{liu_efficientphys} for efficient on-device
inference; the rPPG-Toolbox~\cite{liu_rppgtoolbox} standardizes these. These works report waveform or RR
error, not a clinical decision, and are validated predominantly on adults.

\subsection{Infant and neonatal non-contact monitoring}
Infants differ from adults in skin, motion, breathing rate, and the paucity of labeled data. AIRFlowNet
and the AIR benchmarks~\cite{infant_air} target infant respiration from optical flow, reporting
$\approx$2.9~MAE in-domain. In the neonatal intensive care unit, camera systems have tracked heart and
breathing rate in preterm infants~\cite{villarroel_npj}, and thermal imaging reaches $\approx$1.5~MAE on
neonates~\cite{pereira_thermal}. None of these targets WHO-band tachypnea as the evaluation endpoint.

\subsection{Radar and radio-frequency vital signs}
Doppler and frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar sense sub-millimeter chest motion and are a
mature route to contactless respiration, reviewed extensively in the microwave
literature~\cite{li_radar_tmtt} and among contactless RR methods broadly~\cite{massaroni_review}. Radar
is robust to lighting and skin tone and, as we show, reaches the neonatal band that RGB cannot---but is
rarely evaluated against the pediatric clinical endpoint.

\subsection{Multimodal fusion and sequence models}
Combining modalities can improve robustness when any single channel is unreliable. Recent state-space
models such as Mamba~\cite{mamba_gu} offer linear-time sequence encoding and have been adopted as fusion
backbones. We include a Mamba-style and a CliffPhys-style backbone as architecture comparisons within our
own framework. Respiratory audio is a complementary modality with its own benchmarks~\cite{icbhi_rocha}.

\subsection{Uncertainty, calibration, and selective prediction}
A screening tool must know when to abstain. Conformal prediction~\cite{vovk_alrw,angelopoulos_conformal}
gives distribution-free coverage; selective classification~\cite{geifman_selective} trades coverage for
accuracy; calibration is assessed by the Brier score~\cite{brier} and expected calibration error, and
clinical usefulness by decision-curve analysis~\cite{vickers_dca}. We use an age-conditional (Mondrian)
conformal rule so that the abstention policy respects the neonatal blind spot.

\subsection{Clinical respiratory-rate counting}
The WHO IMCI thresholds~\cite{who_imci} define our labels. Automated counters have been evaluated against
expert video panels for pneumonia, but skin-tone and demographic bias remain a documented concern for
optical methods~\cite{nowara_skintone}, which we discuss as a limitation.

\section{Method}
Our contribution is a \emph{reframing}: rather than regress a respiratory rate and threshold it, we cast
tachypnea as classification of the respiratory signal (Sec.~\ref{sec:respsal}). Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows
the two stages---a shared signal front end followed by a salience classifier---alongside the strong
regression baseline (PRISM-Breath) against which we measure the gain, an age-conditional conformal triage
rule, and a supporting radar path for the neonatal band.

\subsection{Signal extraction}
Per video we extract (i) a monocular-depth respiration proxy from Depth-Anything-V2~\cite{yang_depthanythingv2},
(ii) a learned optical-flow waveform from a body-ROI EfficientPhys+Coarse-to-Fine
extractor~\cite{liu_efficientphys}, and (iii) an audio spectrogram. Signals are resampled to a canonical
$25$~Hz common time base (a step whose absence we found inflates error) and band-limited to the
respiratory band $0.15$--$1.5$~Hz ($9$--$90$~breaths/min).

\subsection{PRISM-Breath: the strong regression baseline}\label{sec:prism}
The regression baseline we improve upon is a reliability-weighted multimodal network. Each modality $m$ is
encoded by a branch with explicit spectral features, a temporal ``scan'' block, and separate trunks,
initialized by self-supervised (masked-signal / contrastive) pretraining, producing a rate estimate
$\hat r_m$ and a learned quality $q_m$. A fusion stage combines the branches by a reliability weight that
multiplies quality, temporal support $s_m$, an inverse scale $\sigma_m$, and a respiratory-band
signal-to-noise ratio, with inter-branch agreement downweighting outliers:
\begin{equation}
w_m \;\propto\; q_m \cdot \frac{s_m}{\sigma_m}\cdot \mathrm{SNR}_m,\qquad
\hat r \;=\; \sum_m \frac{w_m}{\sum_{m'} w_{m'}}\,\hat r_m .
\end{equation}
Regression and tachypnea heads output $\hat r$ and a probability against the WHO age-band label. Two
internal analyses shape this design: a load-bearing audit (Sec.~\ref{sec:abl}) shows a prototype bank, a
clean-breathing codebook, and a heteroscedastic head are inert, so a \emph{lean} variant that removes them
is equivalent; and a component isolation (Sec.~\ref{sec:simple}) shows the per-modality \emph{encoder}, not
the fusion, carries the regression performance. PRISM is a genuinely strong baseline---it beats published
extractors on the clinical endpoint (Sec.~\ref{sec:sota})---but as a regressor it remains bounded by the
neonatal ceiling that our classification reframing (Sec.~\ref{sec:respsal}) removes.

\subsection{Age-conditional conformal triage}
A single pooled conformal interval mis-covers by age because RGB reliability collapses for neonates.
We compute a Mondrian nonconformity quantile $\hat q^{(b)}$ per age band $b$ from validation residuals
and form $[\hat r - \hat q^{(b)}, \hat r + \hat q^{(b)}]$. For $<$2~months this interval is
uninformatively wide, which we operationalize as \emph{defer to clinician}.

\subsection{Radar pipeline}
For the neonatal band we process FMCW in-phase/quadrature signals~\cite{li_radar_tmtt}: the chest-range-bin
phase $\phi(t)=\arg(I+jQ)$ is unwrapped, band-passed to $0.1$--$1.7$~Hz, and its dominant spectral peak
gives RR. We apply the identical estimator to a validated clinical reference on real children.

\subsection{RespSalience: respiratory rate as pitch salience}\label{sec:respsal}
Our analysis (Sec.~\ref{sec:framing}) shows the dominant clinical gain comes not from any component above
but from abandoning rate \emph{regression} altogether. Following fundamental-frequency estimation in
audio~\cite{crepe,spice}, we reframe the task: a compact one-dimensional network maps a modality signal to
a \emph{salience} distribution $s(\cdot)$ over a grid of candidate rates ($6$--$90$~breaths/min,
$0.5$~breaths/min spacing), trained with a Gaussian target centred at the true rate. The rate estimate is
the salience-weighted expectation; the tachypnea probability is the salience mass above the age threshold,
$\hat p = \sum_{r \ge \tau_{\text{age}}} s(r) / \sum_r s(r)$, a calibrated probability without post-hoc
adjustment. Because the target is a distribution over rates rather than a regressed scalar, the
formulation has \emph{no upper ceiling}; it optionally adds a learnable band-pass front-end~\cite{sincnet}
and self-supervised (masked-signal) pretraining. This is the model we recommend for deployment.

\section{Datasets and Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Datasets}
Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the data. \textbf{Pediatric-RR-332} (332 infants, RGB video, WHO-band
tachypnea labels) is the primary clinical benchmark. \textbf{AIR-125/400}~\cite{infant_air} (infant
respiration video) are used for in-domain and cross-dataset evaluation. For radar we use an open thorax
simulator (ToF$+$radar, $20$--$80$~breaths/min) and an open dataset of \textbf{50 real children} with
FMCW radar and a clinically approved reference.

\subsection{Protocol, metrics, and statistics}
We use subject-disjoint five-fold cross-validation so that every subject is tested exactly once and no
subject appears in both train and test. Published extractors are trained and evaluated on the $298$
cached subjects common to all methods, giving a matched paired comparison; PRISM's own five-fold spans
all $332$. We report RR-MAE and, on the clinical endpoint, sensitivity/specificity/AUROC with
subject-level bootstrap $95\%$ confidence intervals~\cite{efron_bootstrap}; paired comparisons use
DeLong~\cite{delong} for AUROC and exact McNemar for the binary decision. Calibration is reported as
expected calibration error (ECE) and Brier score~\cite{brier}, and clinical usefulness by decision-curve
analysis~\cite{vickers_dca}. Headline operating points are averaged over three seeds. PRISM and all ablations share a matched training
budget (100-epoch cap with best-validation-checkpoint selection); every method, including the published
extractors, is selected by its best validation checkpoint (see Supplementary Sec.~S4).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Primary clinical performance and per-fold stability}\label{sec:primary}
Across the five subject-disjoint folds PRISM-Breath attains RR-MAE $6.19\pm1.32$ and tachypnea AUROC
$0.892\pm0.060$ (per-fold mean $\pm$ SD; pooled AUROC $0.879$). Fig.~\ref{fig:perfold}(a) shows the model
is stable across folds, with the hardest fold (F0) at AUROC $0.812$ and the easiest (F2) at $0.969$;
per-fold values are $0.812$, $0.882$, $0.969$, $0.868$, $0.929$. Full-cohort clinical operating points
(three-seed) are in Table~\ref{tab:clin}.

\subsection{Raw MAE is not clinical utility}\label{sec:sota}
Table~\ref{tab:sota} and Figs.~\ref{fig:sota},~\ref{fig:perfold}(b) give the central result. On the
$298$ matched subjects, EfficientPhys, DeepPhys, TS-CAN, and VIRENet all match or beat PRISM on RR-MAE
($5.61$--$6.15$ vs.\ $6.40$) yet are all significantly worse on tachypnea AUROC: PRISM $0.869$ vs.\
$0.727$--$0.752$, DeLong $p=0.0003$--$0.0016$. \emph{The lower-MAE models are the worse screens.} We
report one honest caveat: an exact McNemar test on the binary decision at the default operating point is
\emph{not} significant ($p=0.16$--$0.75$). PRISM thus significantly improves tachypnea
\emph{discrimination}; converting that ranking advantage into a significantly better fixed-threshold
decision is a matter of operating-point selection we leave to deployment (Section~\ref{sec:disc}).

\subsection{Architecture comparisons}
Evaluated within our framework on all $332$ subjects, PRISM's reliability-weighted fusion
(AUROC $0.879$) significantly beats a Mamba-style state-space fusion backbone~\cite{mamba_gu}
($0.811$; DeLong $p=0.0023$; MAE $7.55$) and is statistically comparable to a CliffPhys-style backbone
($0.853$; $p=0.090$; MAE $6.53$). But as Section~\ref{sec:simple} shows, the advantage is not in the deep
head at all---it is in the extracted signal and the training recipe, not the sequence encoder or fusion
depth.

\subsection{Where the performance comes from: a component isolation}\label{sec:simple}
Table~\ref{tab:simple} isolates the source of the clinical signal, evaluating each stage on the same
subject-disjoint folds. The \emph{raw} extracted flow, read out by a plain FFT with no learned encoder,
discriminates tachypnea at only AUROC $0.723$ (stable across band choices)---no better than the end-to-end
EfficientPhys baseline ($0.745$). PRISM's compact per-modality encoder (spectral features, a temporal scan
block, and self-supervised pretraining) lifts this to $0.873$ (paired DeLong $p<10^{-4}$): this is where
the performance is created. The deep fusion head then adds nothing ($0.879$); a plain logistic regression
on the encoder's per-modality outputs matches it ($0.894$; $p=0.23$), and even a linear rule on the single
encoded flow-RR reaches $0.873$. The clinical advantage is thus attributable to the \emph{learned
per-modality encoder}---not the raw extraction (mediocre) and not the fusion architecture (unnecessary),
consistent with the ablation (Table~\ref{tab:abl}). The parsimonious deployable model is therefore
extractor~$\rightarrow$~compact encoder~$\rightarrow$~linear readout.

\subsection{The clinical task is classification, not regression}\label{sec:framing}
Sections~\ref{sec:sota}--\ref{sec:simple} operate within the prevailing paradigm---estimate a rate, then
threshold. Table~\ref{tab:framing} and Fig.~\ref{fig:isolation} show that abandoning it is the single largest gain in
this paper.
Reframing tachypnea as \emph{classification of the raw respiratory signal} (RespSalience,
Sec.~\ref{sec:respsal}) raises AUROC from $0.879$ (regression, full PRISM) to $0.925$ (DeLong $p=0.022$).
The gain is \emph{architecture-agnostic}: a purpose-built salience network ($0.925$), the same network
with self-supervised pretraining ($0.930$), and a generic random-convolution classifier
(ROCKET~\cite{rocket}, $0.915$) are statistically indistinguishable from one another (all pairwise
$p>0.3$) while each significantly beats every regression baseline. The improvement is thus a property of
the framing, not of any particular model---a robust, transferable finding rather than a fragile
architecture claim.

The reframing also attacks the neonatal blind spot. A salience over a rate grid has no regression ceiling,
so the classifier recovers discrimination in the $<$2-month band where regression collapses: AUROC rises
from $0.619$ (PRISM) to $0.881$ (salience) to $0.935$ (with pretraining), and predicted rates track true
rates up to $71$~breaths/min---well past the $\sim$35~breaths/min ceiling of every regressor
(Fig.~\ref{fig:salience}). The neonatal subgroup is small ($n{=}29$, wide confidence intervals), so we
present this as strong supporting evidence, not a definitive neonatal claim. Among the tied classifiers we
recommend the salience network for deployment: unlike ROCKET it is calibrated out of the box (ECE $0.053$,
Brier $0.092$), returns an interpretable rate, and is ceiling-free.

\subsection{Age bands and the neonatal limit}\label{sec:age}
Table~\ref{tab:clin} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ageband} break performance down by WHO band. For the neonatal
cases (true RR $60$--$80$), the extracted flow itself peaks near $35$~breaths/min and every extractor
outputs $\approx$26--40, so neonatal sensitivity is $0.00$ for all configurations
(Fig.~\ref{fig:paradigm}(a)). We therefore defer $<$2~months and report the $\ge$2mo screen: sensitivity
$0.71$ $[0.60,0.80]$, AUROC $0.919$ $[0.881,0.950]$, with the older band (12--60mo) reaching sensitivity
$0.83$ and AUROC $0.950$.

\subsection{Ablations and the lean model}\label{sec:abl}
Table~\ref{tab:abl} lists component contributions under the pooled five-fold protocol. Removing
self-supervised pretraining is the most damaging ($-0.11$ sensitivity, $-0.017$ AUROC); removing spectral
features, the scan block, or separate trunks each costs sensitivity; removing the prototype bank, the
codebook, or the heteroscedastic head changes the clinical endpoint negligibly. PRISM-Breath-Lean
(all three removed) matches the full model (Fig.~\ref{fig:abl}), giving an Occam-clean design. A flow-only
configuration (depth and audio disabled) still beats EfficientPhys on AUROC ($0.860$, $p=0.002$),
localizing the win to the head rather than the extra modalities.

\subsection{Extractor robustness and a retracted result}
The clinical result is robust to the flow backbone: a TS-CAN-derived flow gives AUROC $0.897$ under a
leakage-free per-fold protocol, comparable to PRISM. We flag, and retract, an apparent TS-CAN improvement
(AUROC $0.911$) that we traced to extractor leakage across folds; the leakage-free number is the honest
one. An SNR-gated fusion de-collapses the reliability weights but does not change the clinical endpoint
on this cohort, where the live modalities have similar signal quality.

\subsection{Radar recovers the neonatal band}\label{sec:radar}
Fig.~\ref{fig:paradigm} and Table~\ref{tab:radar} show radar RR is exact on the phantom across
$20$--$80$~breaths/min (MAE $0.00$, $r{=}1.00$) and clinical-grade on $50$ real children (MAE $1.76$,
$r{=}0.96$, $86\%$ within $3$~breaths/min)---precisely where RGB saturates. This is a cross-modality
existence proof: the neonatal information is physically available to radar even though it is not available
to the camera.

\subsection{Cross-dataset generalization}
Trained on Pediatric-RR and evaluated on the external AIR-400 infant cohort, PRISM attains RR-MAE $3.12$,
competitive with the infant-specific SOTA~\cite{infant_air}; in-domain on AIR-400 it reaches $2.93$.
Hand-crafted optical flow collapses out of domain ($11.3$--$11.8$), which justifies the learned flow
channel (Table~\ref{tab:radar}).

\subsection{Calibration, decision curve, and equity}\label{sec:calib}
PRISM is well-calibrated (ECE $0.064$, Brier $0.110$) and, on decision-curve analysis, its net benefit
exceeds both treat-all and treat-none at every threshold (Fig.~\ref{fig:calib}). At a fixed specificity of
$0.90$ the screen retains sensitivity $0.70$; positive predictive value is $0.73$ on this cohort
(prevalence $0.26$) but falls to $0.46$ at a community prevalence of $0.10$, while negative predictive
value stays high ($0.96$)---the screen rules out better than it rules in at low prevalence. We find no
significant
sex disparity (male AUROC $0.890$ vs.\ female $0.871$; MAE gap n.s.). Finally, on the cohort's own
clinical sign (observed difficulty breathing), true RR is only weakly predictive (AUROC $0.59$) and
estimated RR weaker still ($0.54$)---an honest reminder that tachypnea is a screening sign, not a disease
label.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}
\textbf{Metric reframing.} Our central message is methodological: a screen should be judged by its
clinical discrimination and calibration, not by RR-MAE. Four published extractors are better regressors
and worse screens (Section~\ref{sec:sota}). The non-significant McNemar result sharpens rather than
weakens this point: AUROC captures the separability the extractors lack, while the fixed-threshold
decision is dominated by the shared difficulty of the cohort near the boundary; the practical implication
is that deployment should tune the operating point (e.g., to a target sensitivity) and re-estimate the
decision-level benefit prospectively.

\textbf{What actually matters.} A component isolation (Section~\ref{sec:simple}) locates the clinical
performance precisely. It is \emph{not} the raw extraction: the learned flow, read out by FFT, discriminates
tachypnea at only $0.72$, no better than an end-to-end video CNN. It is \emph{not} the deep fusion head: a
calibrated linear model on the encoder's per-modality outputs is statistically indistinguishable from the
full network. It \emph{is} the compact, self-supervised spectral-temporal encoder, which lifts the raw
signal from $0.72$ to $0.87$ ($p<10^{-4}$) and is exactly what the ablation flags as load-bearing. For a
low-resource setting this is an actionable design rule: invest in the per-modality encoder and pretraining,
keep the readout linear and calibratable, and do not add fusion machinery that a logistic regression
already subsumes.

\textbf{Clinical translation.} A sensitivity of $0.71$ for the $\ge$2mo screen is not a stand-alone
diagnosis; it is a triage signal that, combined with mandatory neonatal referral and calibrated
abstention, can prioritize scarce clinician attention. Calibration and positive net benefit
(Section~\ref{sec:calib}) are what make such a signal safe to act on.

\textbf{The neonatal limit and radar.} The RGB saturation near $35$~breaths/min is physical, not a tuning
artifact, and it affects every extractor we tested; hiding it in a pooled average would be the less honest
choice. Radar removes the ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:radar}). The integration is not yet complete: our
radar and RGB validations are on different cohorts, so an integrated RGB$+$radar device on the same
infants---especially real neonates in cribs---is the natural next step.

\textbf{Comparison to published numbers.} Infant-specific methods report $\approx$2.9~RR-MAE in
domain~\cite{infant_air}; we reproduce comparable MAE ($2.93$ on AIR-400) but show that such MAE does not
by itself deliver tachypnea discrimination on a clinical cohort, which is the quantity a screen needs.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i) The clinical cohort is single-site with manual-count ground truth; a second
site and a physiological reference (capnography or respiratory-inductance plethysmography) are needed.
(ii) Skin-tone equity is not auditable in a single-population cohort, a documented risk for optical
methods~\cite{nowara_skintone}. (iii) Tachypnea is a weak proxy for disease (AUROC $0.59$ for difficulty
breathing). (iv) The radar arm is validated on a phantom and on real children, but not yet on real
neonates. (v)~We benchmark against WHO manual breath counting, itself subject to inter-rater error of
several breaths/min, which likely caps achievable discrimination near the threshold and contributes to the
non-significant McNemar result; separately, a small number ($15$) of validation signals in an earlier
pipeline used a hand-crafted extractor, affecting only checkpoint selection---all reported \emph{test}
metrics use the learned extractor on held-out folds. These define a prospective, outcome-anchored,
multi-modal study.

\section{Conclusion}
PRISM-Breath is a calibrated, ablation-justified, non-contact tachypnea screen for infants
$\ge$2~months, and it beats four published extractors on the clinical endpoint precisely where raw-error
metrics say it should not. Because RGB cannot see neonatal rates, we make the referral policy explicit and
show that radar closes the gap on real children---charting a sensing-modality route to the group that
matters most. We advocate that non-contact respiration work report the clinical endpoint, calibration,
and age-stratified performance, not RR-MAE alone.
