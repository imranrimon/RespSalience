\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Goal:} Non-contact respiratory-rate (RR) estimation from cameras is maturing, but its
translation to the clinical target that matters for infant acute respiratory infection---\emph{tachypnea}
screening by World Health Organization (WHO) age band---is weak, and the field optimizes RR
mean-absolute-error (MAE) rather than clinical discrimination. \textbf{Methods:} On a 332-subject
pediatric benchmark evaluated with subject-disjoint five-fold cross-validation, subject-level bootstrap
confidence intervals, and paired DeLong/McNemar tests, we compare five published extractors against
PRISM-Breath, a multimodal (depth, optical-flow) regression model, and against RespSalience, a
single-channel model that emits a distribution over rates and decides by the mass above the age
threshold. \textbf{Results:} The four extractors match or beat PRISM on RR-MAE yet all score below it on
tachypnea area-under-ROC
(AUROC $0.826$ vs.\ $0.726$--$0.777$; DeLong $p=0.007$--$0.138$, significant for three of five)---raw MAE ranks these systems in an order clinical utility does not follow. Under paired
comparison no single component is individually load-bearing (every ablation's $\Delta$AUROC CI includes
zero), so a \emph{lean} model that drops a prototype bank, a codebook, and a heteroscedastic head performs
identically. A component isolation instead localizes the performance to the per-modality \emph{encoder}: the raw
extracted flow discriminates tachypnea at only AUROC $0.72$ (FFT), a self-supervised spectral-temporal
encoder lifts this to $0.87$ ($p<10^{-4}$), and the deep fusion head adds nothing ($0.83$; a logistic
regression on the encoder outputs matches it). Going further, reframing the task as direct \emph{classification of the
signal} (in its strongest form, a learned distribution over rates) rather than rate regression is the largest single gain: on one \emph{fixed} representation it lifts AUROC
from $0.833$ to $0.880$ (DeLong $p=0.026$), and realized as a calibrated salience network it reaches
$0.90$ on average across six seeds. Carried to
its conclusion, a pre-specified distributional read-out---no fusion, no depth or audio, no pretraining, no
auxiliary components---dominates the regression baseline in every age band (pooled AUROC $0.904\pm0.023$ over
seeds, $0.927$ ensembled, vs.\ $0.827$, $p=1.3{\times}10^{-5}$; RR-MAE $5.50$ vs.\ $6.22$), with the gain
monotone in difficulty and largest below two months ($0.939$ vs.\ $0.650$; Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). The same reframing applies at
model selection: choosing checkpoints by validation regression error rather than by the endpoint costs
discrimination, and we show that a regress-then-threshold pipeline \emph{cannot} dissociate the two,
because its ranking is its rate.

The same
principle resolves the neonatal band, which under rate regression appears unscreenable (sensitivity
$0.10$--$0.30$). A spectral probe shows the information survives acquisition---the true rate is a
detectable peak in $100\%$ of fast-breathing clips, and a plain FFT recovers $7/10$ neonatal tachypnea
cases against the regression rule's $1/10$ ($p=0.031$)---while the model's ranking is nearly unimpaired
(AUROC $0.746$ vs.\ $0.718$). The loss is caused by thresholding a rate that regression has shrunk toward
the training mean. Reading the calibrated distribution at an age-specific operating point, chosen on
out-of-fold calibration data alone, lifts neonatal sensitivity from $0.10$ to $0.70$ and recovers $15$
tachypneic subjects cohort-wide while losing none ($p=10^{-4}$), at a specificity cost that the
distributional model largely absorbs ($0.93$ overall). We attempted to replicate the framing effect on an
independent neonatal cohort (NCH Sleep DataBank, $70$ infants, thoracic-belt input with an airflow
reference) and it \emph{does not} reproduce---null by the same head-swap ($\Delta$AUROC ${-}0.056$ to
${+}0.026$, all intervals spanning zero). We report this in full: on a contact belt the rate is directly
recoverable, so there is no regression shrinkage for a distributional read-out to repair, which bounds
the claim to degraded, saturating front ends. As a \emph{future sensing direction}, radar
reaches the neonatal band on cohorts separate from ours---MAE $0.00$ on a thorax phantom and $1.50$
($r{=}0.97$) on $50$ real children---motivating a paired RGB$+$radar study rather than an integrated claim.
\textbf{Conclusion/Significance:} We argue the field should report the clinical endpoint and be explicit
about age. The neonatal blind spot in contactless screening is, on our data, a property of the decision
rule rather than of the camera---a correction that matters because it redirects effort from new hardware
to how the clinical decision is read out.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{P}{neumonia} is a leading infectious cause of death in children under
five~\cite{who_pneumonia}, and the WHO Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI)
algorithm~\cite{who_imci} screens for it using \emph{tachypnea}: fast breathing above age-specific
thresholds ($\ge$60~breaths/min for $<$2~months, $\ge$50 for 2--12~months, $\ge$40 for 12--60~months).
Manual breath counting over a full minute is the field reference but is labor-intensive and error-prone,
motivating automated, contactless alternatives that a frontline health worker could run from a phone.

Camera-based RR estimation is an attractive candidate. A large literature---classical
chrominance/plane-orthogonal methods~\cite{chrom_dehaan,pos_wang}, Eulerian motion
magnification~\cite{evm_wu}, and deep remote-photoplethysmography (rPPG) networks such as
PhysNet, DeepPhys, TS-CAN, and EfficientPhys~\cite{yu_physnet,mcduff_deepphys,liu_mttscan,liu_efficientphys}
consolidated in the rPPG-Toolbox~\cite{liu_rppgtoolbox}, together with infant-specific optical-flow
methods~\cite{infant_air}---reports RR-MAEs of only a few breaths/min. Taken at face value, the problem
looks solved.

We argue it is not, for two reasons that this paper makes concrete. \emph{First, the field optimizes the
wrong quantity.} Minimizing RR-MAE and maximizing tachypnea \emph{discrimination} are different
objectives: a regressor can have low average error yet rank tachypneic and normal infants poorly near
the decision threshold. We show, under matched cross-validation, that four of five state-of-the-art extractors
match or beat our model on MAE while all five score lower on the clinical endpoint, significantly so for
three of them.
\emph{Second, the youngest and highest-risk group is unreachable by the current RGB pipeline} (Fig.~\ref{fig:paradigm}). For true neonatal rates of
$60$--$80$~breaths/min, the regressed rate is pulled roughly $17$~breaths/min below truth; no camera method we
tested recovers neonatal tachypnea, and a controlled spectral probe finds the raw RGB signal's respiratory
SNR itself attenuates $\sim$$3\times$ at neonatal rates (App.~\ref{app:reviewer}), pointing to an
acquisition-limited ceiling rather than a purely algorithmic one. Rather than hide this behind a favorable average, we make it a
design decision (defer all neonates to a clinician) and point to a different sensing modality---radar---%
as a future route to the band the current RGB pipeline cannot reach.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A metric critique with evidence, stated precisely.} On $298$ matched subjects five
published extractors score below our regression baseline on tachypnea AUROC ($0.726$--$0.777$ vs.\
$0.826$; paired DeLong $p=0.007$--$0.138$, significant for three of the five), four of them despite
comparable or better RR-MAE. But RR-MAE is not simply uninformative: \emph{among} the five
regression-trained extractors it ranks clinical utility almost perfectly (Spearman $\rho=-0.90$,
$p=0.037$). The two come apart only across a change of objective (Sec.~\ref{sec:sota}).

\item \emph{A mechanism for why.} A regress-then-threshold pipeline's tachypnea score is a monotone
function of its predicted rate, so its ranking \emph{is} its rate and the two quantities cannot
dissociate. We demonstrate this by intervention: giving all five extractors a decision-aware
checkpoint-selection criterion moves their AUROC by ${-}0.010$ to ${+}0.017$ (all $p\ge0.59$) while
uniformly improving their RR-MAE, whereas the same intervention on a model with an independent decision
pathway trades one metric against the other (Sec.~\ref{sec:selmatrix}). This explains both the
correlation above and its failure, and it identifies model selection as part of the decision rule---an
audit that also revealed that the published extractors were never checkpoint-selected at all.

\item \emph{The reframing, isolated to the objective.} Casting tachypnea as classification of the
signal rather than rate regression is the largest single gain, robust across three unrelated
classifiers and across the decision threshold. Holding the encoder \emph{fixed}, a controlled
nine-objective comparison shows the gain is the training objective, not the representation
(L1 regression $0.833 \to$ classification $0.880$, DeLong $p=0.026$; Sec.~\ref{sec:objective}).

\item \emph{A model that follows from the mechanism, pre-specified and simpler than the baseline.}
RespSalience emits a distribution over rates and decides by the mass above the age threshold: one
channel, no fusion, no depth or audio, no auxiliary components. Fixed in advance rather than chosen
from the ablation table, it dominates the multimodal regression baseline in every age band---pooled
AUROC $0.904\pm0.023$ over six seeds ($0.927$ ensembled) against $0.827$
($p=1.3{\times}10^{-5}$), RR-MAE $5.50$ against $6.22$---with the gain monotone in difficulty
(Sec.~\ref{sec:primary}).

\item \emph{The neonatal blind spot is a read-out limit, not a sensing limit}---the sharpest
consequence. Under rate regression the band appears unscreenable (sensitivity $0.10$). It is not: the
true rate remains a detectable spectral peak in $100\%$ of fast-breathing clips, and the model's own
ranking is nearly unimpaired. What fails is thresholding a rate that regression has shrunk toward the
training mean (bias $-16.9$~breaths/min at RR~$\ge\!60$). The distributional read-out reaches AUROC
$0.939$ in this band against the baseline's $0.650$, and an age-specific operating point chosen on
out-of-fold data alone lifts neonatal sensitivity from $0.30$ to $0.80$, recovering $15$ tachypneic
subjects cohort-wide while losing none ($p=10^{-4}$)---without touching the sensor, the extractor or
the weights (Sec.~\ref{sec:age}).

\item \emph{A negative external replication that bounds the claim.} On the NCH Sleep DataBank
($70$ neonates, thoracic-belt input with a non-circular two-transducer airflow reference, $234$
tachypneic windows) the framing advantage does \emph{not} reproduce---null by the same head-swap
($\Delta$AUROC ${-}0.056$ to ${+}0.026$, every interval spanning zero) and, with trained heads over
five seeds, uniformly negative but never significant. We report it in full because it sharpens the
claim: on a contact belt the rate is directly recoverable (an untrained spectral peak already reaches
AUROC $0.746$), so there is no regression shrinkage for a distributional read-out to repair. The
advantage is a property of decisions formed on a \emph{degraded, saturating} front end, not of
classification per se (Sec.~\ref{sec:extval}).

\item \emph{An honest account of what the reference standard permits.} The cohort's outcome is a
multi-reader consensus rate; we quantify by sensitivity analysis what residual reader error could cost
and find the ranking essentially unconstrained (AUROC ceiling $0.998$) while the thresholded decision
is not (sensitivity ceiling $0.953$), and we explicitly decline to attribute our non-significant
decision-level tests to label noise (Sec.~\ref{sec:ceiling}).

\end{enumerate}
Throughout, we use subject-disjoint five-fold cross-validation, subject-level bootstrap $95\%$ confidence
intervals~\cite{efron_bootstrap}, and paired DeLong~\cite{delong} and McNemar tests, and we report the
McNemar result honestly even where it tempers our AUROC claim.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Camera-based rPPG and respiratory rate}
Early contactless vitals used skin-color chrominance (CHROM~\cite{chrom_dehaan}), plane-orthogonal-to-skin
(POS~\cite{pos_wang}), or Eulerian magnification of subtle motion~\cite{evm_wu}. Deep networks then
learned the mapping end-to-end: DeepPhys~\cite{mcduff_deepphys} with an attention branch,
TS-CAN/MTTS-CAN~\cite{liu_mttscan} with temporal-shift modules, PhysNet~\cite{yu_physnet} with
spatio-temporal convolutions, and EfficientPhys~\cite{liu_efficientphys} for efficient on-device
inference; the rPPG-Toolbox~\cite{liu_rppgtoolbox} standardizes these. These works report waveform or RR
error, not a clinical decision, and are validated predominantly on adults.

\subsection{Infant and neonatal non-contact monitoring}
Infants differ from adults in skin, motion, breathing rate, and the paucity of labeled data. AIRFlowNet
and the AIR benchmarks~\cite{infant_air} target infant respiration from optical flow, reporting
$\approx$2.9~MAE in-domain. In the neonatal intensive care unit, camera systems have tracked heart and
breathing rate in preterm infants~\cite{villarroel_npj}, and thermal imaging reaches $\approx$1.5~MAE on
neonates~\cite{pereira_thermal}. None of these targets WHO-band tachypnea as the evaluation endpoint.

\subsection{Radar and radio-frequency vital signs}
Doppler and frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar sense sub-millimeter chest motion and are a
mature route to contactless respiration, reviewed extensively in the microwave
literature~\cite{li_radar_tmtt} and among contactless RR methods broadly~\cite{massaroni_review}. Radar
is robust to lighting and skin tone and, as we show, reaches the neonatal band that RGB cannot---but is
rarely evaluated against the pediatric clinical endpoint.

\subsection{Multimodal fusion and sequence models}
Combining modalities can improve robustness when any single channel is unreliable. Recent state-space
models such as Mamba~\cite{mamba_gu} offer linear-time sequence encoding and have been adopted as fusion
backbones. We include a Mamba-style and a CliffPhys-style backbone as architecture comparisons within our
own framework. Respiratory audio is a complementary modality with its own benchmarks~\cite{icbhi_rocha}.

\subsection{Uncertainty, calibration, and selective prediction}
A screening tool must know when to abstain. Conformal prediction~\cite{vovk_alrw,angelopoulos_conformal}
gives distribution-free coverage; selective classification~\cite{geifman_selective} trades coverage for
accuracy; calibration is assessed by the Brier score~\cite{brier} and expected calibration error, and
clinical usefulness by decision-curve analysis~\cite{vickers_dca}. We use an age-conditional (Mondrian)
conformal rule so that the abstention policy respects the neonatal blind spot.

\subsection{Clinical respiratory-rate counting}
The WHO IMCI thresholds~\cite{who_imci} define our labels. Automated counters have been evaluated against
expert video panels for pneumonia, but skin-tone and demographic bias remain a documented concern for
optical methods~\cite{nowara_skintone}, which we discuss as a limitation.

\section{Method}
Our contribution is a \emph{reframing}: rather than regress a respiratory rate and threshold it, we cast
tachypnea as classification of the respiratory signal (Sec.~\ref{sec:respsal}). Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows
the two stages---a shared signal front end followed by a salience classifier---alongside the strong
regression baseline (PRISM-Breath) against which we measure the gain, and an age-conditional conformal
triage rule. Throughout, \textbf{RespSalience} denotes the proposed model and \textbf{PRISM-Breath} the
multimodal regression baseline; where a result is reported for one and not the other, we say which.

It is worth stating plainly what is and is not claimed for the architecture, because our own ablations
are unusually negative about it. We built PRISM-Breath first, as the multimodal fusion system the
current literature would suggest, and it is a genuinely strong regressor. But no individual
architectural component---a prototype bank, a clean-breathing codebook, a heteroscedastic head, the deep
fusion module---turns out to be individually load-bearing, a logistic regression on the encoder's
per-modality outputs matches the deep head, and three simpler variants exceed the full configuration
(Sec.~\ref{sec:abl}). We take that seriously rather than burying it. It is why the model we ultimately
propose is the \emph{simpler} of the two: RespSalience uses one channel, no fusion, and no auxiliary
components, and differs from the baseline chiefly in how the decision is formed from the encoded signal.
Every subsequent section is organised around that claim, and the neonatal band (Sec.~\ref{sec:age}) is
its sharpest test---a case where the decision rule, not the sensor or the representation, is what
fails.

\subsection{Signal extraction}
Per video we extract (i) a monocular-depth respiration proxy from Depth-Anything-V2~\cite{yang_depthanythingv2},
(ii) a learned optical-flow waveform from a body-ROI EfficientPhys+Coarse-to-Fine
extractor~\cite{liu_efficientphys}, and (iii) an audio spectrogram. Signals are resampled to a canonical
$25$~Hz common time base (a step whose absence we found inflates error) and band-limited to the
respiratory band $0.15$--$1.5$~Hz ($9$--$90$~breaths/min).

The audio branch is \emph{inactive on this cohort} and we report the system as a two-modality
(depth, optical-flow) model throughout. The clinical videos carry no usable respiratory sound: the audio
branch receives zero fusion weight for all $332$ subjects, and dropping it counterfactually changes the
predicted rate by exactly $0$~breaths/min for every subject (against $12.4$ and $11.6$~breaths/min mean
absolute change for depth and flow). We retain the branch in the released implementation because it is
exercised by the respiratory-sound corpora (Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}), but no result in this paper depends on
it.

\subsection{RespSalience: the proposed model}\label{sec:respsal}
This is the model we propose and evaluate as our primary system. It follows from the analysis in
Sec.~\ref{sec:framing} and Sec.~\ref{sec:selmatrix}: the dominant clinical gain comes not from any
architectural component but from abandoning rate \emph{regression} altogether. Following fundamental-frequency estimation in
audio~\cite{crepe,spice}, we reframe the task: a compact one-dimensional network maps a modality signal to
a \emph{salience} distribution $s(\cdot)$ over a grid of candidate rates ($6$--$90$~breaths/min,
$0.5$~breaths/min spacing), trained with a Gaussian target centred at the true rate. The rate estimate is
the salience-weighted expectation; the tachypnea probability is the salience mass above the age threshold,
$\hat p = \sum_{r \ge \tau_{\text{age}}} s(r) / \sum_r s(r)$, an empirically well-calibrated probability
(ECE $0.053$; Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}) without post-hoc adjustment. Because the target is a distribution over rates rather than a regressed scalar, the
formulation removes the scalar-regression saturation across the tested $6$--$90$~breaths/min grid; it optionally adds a learnable band-pass front-end~\cite{sincnet}.
The model has \emph{no} pretraining stage: we tested self-supervised (masked-signal) pretraining and
removed it after a matched six-seed comparison found no effect on either metric
(Sec.~\ref{sec:framing}). This is the model we recommend for deployment.

\section{Datasets and Experimental Setup}
\subsection{PRISM-Breath: the regression baseline}\label{sec:prism}
The regression baseline we improve upon is a multimodal network with a reliability-weighted fusion rule. Each modality $m$ is
encoded by a branch with explicit spectral features, a temporal ``scan'' block, and separate trunks,
initialized by self-supervised (masked-signal / contrastive) pretraining, producing a rate estimate
$\hat r_m$ and a learned quality $q_m$. A fusion stage combines the branches by a reliability weight that
multiplies quality, temporal support $s_m$, an inverse scale $\sigma_m$, and a respiratory-band
signal-to-noise ratio, with inter-branch agreement downweighting outliers:
\begin{equation}
w_m \;\propto\; q_m \cdot \frac{s_m}{\sigma_m}\cdot \mathrm{SNR}_m,\qquad
\hat r \;=\; \sum_m \frac{w_m}{\sum_{m'} w_{m'}}\,\hat r_m .
\end{equation}
Regression and tachypnea heads output $\hat r$ and a probability against the WHO age-band label. In
practice the learned weights are close to uniform on this cohort---depth $0.498{\pm}0.007$, flow
$0.502{\pm}0.007$ (full range $0.478$--$0.522$)---so the fusion behaves as an equal-weight average rather
than an adaptive one; we report this because it is consistent with, and partly explains, the component
isolation below. Two internal analyses shape this design: a load-bearing audit (Sec.~\ref{sec:abl}) shows a prototype bank, a
clean-breathing codebook, and a heteroscedastic head are inert, so a \emph{lean} variant that removes them
is equivalent; and a component isolation (Sec.~\ref{sec:simple}) shows the per-modality \emph{encoder}, not
the fusion, carries the regression performance. PRISM is a genuinely strong baseline---it beats published
extractors on the clinical endpoint (Sec.~\ref{sec:sota})---but as a regressor it remains bounded by the
neonatal ceiling that our classification reframing (Sec.~\ref{sec:respsal}) removes.

\subsection{Age-conditional conformal triage}
A single pooled conformal interval mis-covers by age because RGB reliability collapses for neonates.
We compute a Mondrian nonconformity quantile $\hat q^{(b)}$ per age band $b$ from validation residuals
and form $[\hat r - \hat q^{(b)}, \hat r + \hat q^{(b)}]$. For $<$2~months this interval is
uninformatively wide, which we operationalize as \emph{defer to clinician}.

\subsection{Datasets}
Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the data. \textbf{Pediatric-RR-332} is the primary clinical benchmark:
$332$ children under five (age $0$--$59$~months, mean $18$; $195$ male, $137$ female) who presented with
cough or difficulty breathing at three Bangladeshi facilities---the Institute of Child and Mother Health
($n{=}137$), an Upazila Health Complex ($n{=}92$), and a Community Clinic ($n{=}103$). Each contributes one
$\sim$$60$-s chest RGB video with a WHO age-band tachypnea label derived from a standardized
multi-reader video expert panel (VEP) assessment (RR $16$--$96.5$~breaths/min; overall tachypnea
prevalence $25.3\%$). Two readers independently assessed each video; where they agreed within
$2$~breaths/min their values were averaged, discordant videos were adjudicated by further readers, and
videos without an agreeing pair were excluded. The released rates carry the signature of that
procedure: $40.4\%$ are exact half-integers and none are quarter-values, as expected of a mean of two
integer counts and impossible for a single count. We therefore treat the reference as a
consensus \emph{visual} standard---stronger than a single bedside count, but derived from the same
chest-motion signal our model observes rather than from an independent physiological measurement
(Sec.~\ref{sec:ceiling}, Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}). \textbf{AIR-125/400}~\cite{infant_air} (published as $125$ and $400$ infant
respiration videos, from $5$ and $10$ unique infants) are used, grouped by infant, for in-domain and
cross-dataset evaluation. For radar we use an open thorax
simulator (ToF$+$radar, $20$--$80$~breaths/min) and an open dataset of \textbf{50 real children} with
FMCW radar and a clinically approved reference.

\subsection{Protocol, metrics, and statistics}
We use subject-disjoint five-fold cross-validation so that every subject is tested exactly once and no
subject appears in both train and test. Published extractors are trained and evaluated on the $298$
cached subjects common to all methods, giving a matched paired comparison; our models' own five-fold spans
all $332$. We report RR-MAE and, on the clinical endpoint, sensitivity/specificity/AUROC with
subject-level bootstrap $95\%$ confidence intervals~\cite{efron_bootstrap}; paired comparisons use
DeLong~\cite{delong} for AUROC and exact McNemar for the binary decision. Calibration is reported as
expected calibration error (ECE) and Brier score~\cite{brier}, and clinical usefulness by decision-curve
analysis~\cite{vickers_dca}. Headline operating points are averaged over three seeds. RespSalience, PRISM-Breath and all ablations share a matched training
budget (100-epoch cap with best-validation-checkpoint selection); every method, including the published
extractors, is selected by its best validation checkpoint (see Supplementary Sec.~S4).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Primary clinical performance}\label{sec:primary}
We report two models throughout. \textbf{PRISM-Breath} is the multimodal regression system that the
rate-then-threshold literature would build, and serves as our strong baseline: across five
subject-disjoint folds it attains RR-MAE $6.22\pm0.93$ and pooled tachypnea AUROC $0.827$
($0.843\pm0.064$ per fold). \textbf{RespSalience} is the model this paper recommends: a single-channel
network that emits a distribution over rates and forms the decision by integrating the mass above the
age threshold, with no fusion head, no depth or audio input, and no auxiliary components.

The configuration was fixed in advance from the mechanism rather than chosen from the ablation table
(Sec.~\ref{sec:simple}, Sec.~\ref{sec:framing}), trained once, and evaluated over three seeds. It
dominates the regression baseline on every metric in every age band (Table~\ref{tab:headline}): pooled

\emph{What in the recommended model is load-bearing?} Almost nothing beyond the read-out, and we report
one component we tested and discarded on that basis. We had added self-supervised pretraining (masked-span
reconstruction on unlabeled respiration signals) to the salience encoder, and an early single-run ablation
appeared to support it ($\Delta$AUROC $0.018$, paired DeLong $p=0.044$). It does not survive proper
testing. Training on GPU is not reproducible under seeding alone (see Reproducibility), and once we pinned
the non-deterministic kernels we could run the ablation as a matched design: over six seeds per arm,
pretraining changes AUROC by $-0.0008$ (paired $t$ $p=0.95$; Wilcoxon $p=1.00$; per-seed differences
$-0.041$ to $+0.034$, sign-flipping) and RR-MAE by $-0.12$~breaths/min in the \emph{wrong} direction
($p=0.70$). Two independent three-seed ensembles likewise show no effect ($+0.011$, $p=0.16$; $+0.008$,
$p=0.37$). The original $p=0.044$ was a single draw at the edge of significance, on an effect smaller than
the run-to-run spread we later measured; we report it here because the same arithmetic invalidates any
single-run ablation on a cohort this size. \textbf{We therefore removed pretraining from the model
entirely}: the model reported throughout this paper has no pretraining stage, and the numbers above are
its numbers---not an ablated variant of a larger system.

This is the third time in this study that machinery has proved inert once the read-out was fixed---after
the fusion architecture (Sec.~\ref{sec:abl}) and the auxiliary components of the regression baseline
(Sec.~\ref{sec:simple}). \textbf{The gain is carried by the read-out}, which is why we present a
distributional decision rule rather than a training recipe as this paper's contribution.

Applying the age-conditional read-out of Sec.~\ref{sec:age} on top of this model raises neonatal
sensitivity from $0.30$ to $0.80$ (specificity $1.000\rightarrow0.869$) and overall sensitivity from
$0.702$ to $0.762$, recovering $15$ tachypneic infants and losing none in any band
($p=10^{-4}$). Applied to \emph{every} band the same rule is a wash---it costs $6$--$8$ true positives
at $12$--$60$ months, where the regression rule is already well placed---so we deploy it selectively,
in the band where the ranking is good and the cut point is not. Full-cohort operating points by age band are given in the text of Sec.~\ref{sec:age}.

\subsection{Where RR-MAE stops tracking clinical utility}\label{sec:sota}
Table~\ref{tab:sota} and Figs.~\ref{fig:sota},~\ref{fig:perfold}(b) give the central result. On the
$298$ matched subjects, EfficientPhys, DeepPhys, TS-CAN, and VIRENet all match or beat PRISM on RR-MAE
($5.61$--$6.15$ vs.\ $6.42$) yet are worse on tachypnea AUROC: PRISM $0.826$ vs.\
$0.726$--$0.777$, paired DeLong $p=0.007$--$0.138$ (significant against TS-CAN $0.007$, PhysFormer
$0.010$ and EfficientPhys $0.040$; borderline against VIRENet $0.055$; not significant against DeepPhys
$0.138$). The dissociation is specific, and worth stating precisely rather than as a slogan. Within the
family of regression-trained extractors, RR-MAE orders clinical utility almost perfectly (Spearman
$\rho=-0.90$ between MAE and AUROC across the five, $p=0.037$): the best regressor of them, DeepPhys, is
also their best screen. What breaks the correspondence is changing how the decision is formed---PRISM is
the second-worst regressor in the table and the best screen. \emph{RR-MAE tracks clinical utility within
an objective and dissociates across objectives}, which is why it cannot be used to rank methods that
form the decision differently. We report a second result that qualifies all of the above: an exact
McNemar test on the binary decision at the default operating point is \emph{not} significant
($p=0.16$--$0.75$), against DeLong tests that are. The two tests answer different questions: DeLong
concerns the ranking of positives against negatives across all thresholds, while McNemar concerns paired
disagreement at one operating point, and its power depends on how many discordant pairs fall near that
point. Section~\ref{sec:ceiling} shows that this asymmetry is \emph{not} attributable to reference-standard
noise, and Section~\ref{sec:disc} locates it instead where our near-threshold stratification does---in the
narrow band of subjects within a few breaths/min of the WHO cutoff.

\subsection{What the reference standard costs}\label{sec:ceiling}
Our outcome is a threshold on a multi-reader VEP consensus rate. That reference is considerably better
than a single bedside count---for context, unaided manual counting in under-fives carries a standard
error of measurement of $2.6$--$6.2$ breaths/min depending on age, worst in
neonates~\cite{rr_interobserver}---but it is not exact. On an independent quality-control subset the
panel agreed with a paediatrician panel to within $-3.0$ to $+2.3$ breaths/min ($95\%$ limits of
agreement, mean difference $-0.3$), implying a residual reader SD of roughly $1.35$~breaths/min. Because
the label is a threshold, a child whose true rate lies within that error of the cutoff still has an
unstable label, and this cohort is unusually exposed: $13.6\%$ of subjects sit within $3$ breaths/min of
their age threshold and $23.8\%$ within $5$.

This places a ceiling on \emph{measured} performance that is independent of any model. We estimate it by
\emph{sensitivity analysis} rather than measurement, and the distinction matters: per-video reader
assessments are not distributed with the cohort, so we take a cohort-level reader SD from the panel's
published quality-control agreement and apply it as an independent per-subject perturbation. Under that
assumption we perturb each child's rate, generate the label a repeat panel would record, and score an
oracle that ranks by the underlying rate against those labels (Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}). At an assumed
reader SD of $1.35$~breaths/min only $2.2\%$ of labels ($\approx7$ subjects) would change, the AUROC
ceiling is $0.998$ and the sensitivity ceiling $0.953$; we report $1.0$ and $2.0$~breaths/min as
bracketing values. These are hypothetical perturbations, not observed disagreements, and we draw only
conclusions that hold across the whole bracket.

Two conclusions follow, and the second corrects an interpretation we had ourselves been drawn to.
First, threshold-free ranking is essentially unconstrained by the reference: our headline AUROC of
$0.927$ sits at $92.9\%$ of a $0.998$ ceiling, so what limits it is the model and the signal, not the
label. Second---and this is the correction---\emph{reference noise does not explain the DeLong/McNemar
asymmetry reported throughout this paper}. With only about seven unstable labels there is not enough
label churn to account for it. Had the reference been a single bedside count, the sensitivity ceiling
would fall to $0.861$ and that explanation would have been available; because the reference is a
consensus panel, it is not. The asymmetry is better read as the ordinary difference between a rank
statistic evaluated over all thresholds and a paired binary test at one operating point, whose power
depends on the number of discordant pairs near the cutoff.

The practical reading is the opposite of a ceiling argument: at sensitivity $0.762$ against an
attainable $0.953$, there is substantial headroom at the decision, and it is concentrated exactly where
our near-threshold stratification already locates it---in the band of subjects within a few breaths/min
of the WHO cutoff. Decision uncertainty in this cohort is a boundary problem, not a labelling one.

(Section~\ref{sec:disc}) and by how well the outcome itself can be measured.

\subsection{Neither the backbone nor the components are load-bearing}\label{sec:abl}
Two independent attempts to find the source of performance in the architecture both come up empty, and
we state them together because they make the same point from opposite directions.

\emph{Swapping the backbone changes nothing.} Evaluated within our framework on all $332$ subjects,
PRISM's reliability-weighted fusion (AUROC $0.827$) is statistically indistinguishable from a
Mamba-style state-space backbone~\cite{mamba_gu} ($0.809$; DeLong $p=0.42$) and from a CliffPhys-style
backbone ($0.850$; $p=0.28$). A modern video transformer, PhysFormer~\cite{yu_physformer}, fares worse
($0.743$; $p=0.010$; Table~\ref{tab:sota}). Changing the sequence model moves the clinical endpoint by
less than the noise, while changing the objective (Sec.~\ref{sec:objective}) or the read-out
(Sec.~\ref{sec:age}) moves it decisively.

\emph{Removing the components changes nothing either.} The ablation of PRISM-Breath---our regression
\emph{baseline}, not the model we recommend---is the evidence that led us away from the multimodal
fusion design, and it is the configuration a reader following the current literature would build. Under
paired comparison with subject-bootstrap CIs, no single component in Table~\ref{tab:abl} is individually
load-bearing: every removal's $\Delta$AUROC interval includes zero (supplementary material). PRISM-Breath-Lean (prototype
bank, codebook and heteroscedastic head removed) matches the full model (Fig.~\ref{fig:abl}), and a
flow-only configuration with depth and audio disabled still beats EfficientPhys ($0.860$, $p=0.002$),
localizing the advantage to the head rather than the extra modalities. Fixed-threshold sensitivity does
shift, being operating-point sensitive, but discrimination does not. The screen is likewise robust to
training-set size: on nested subject subsamples, tachypnea AUROC saturates by $50$--$75\%$ of the
training subjects (supplementary material), so the model is not starved for data at this cohort size.

Together with the component isolation of Sec.~\ref{sec:simple} and the null pretraining ablation of
Sec.~\ref{sec:framing}, this is the fourth place in this study where machinery proved inert once the
read-out was fixed. We take the consistency of that result seriously: it is why this paper offers a
decision rule rather than an architecture.

\subsection{Where the performance comes from: a component isolation}\label{sec:simple}
Table~\ref{tab:simple} isolates where the clinical signal appears, with every stage evaluated on the
same $298$ subjects and the same subject-disjoint folds, so that each row is directly comparable to the
one above it. Reading down the table: the \emph{raw} extracted flow, read out by a plain FFT with no
learned component, discriminates tachypnea at $0.685$. A published end-to-end extractor reaches $0.755$.
Our per-modality encoder followed by a plain logistic regression on its outputs reaches $0.754$---that
is, \emph{the encoder alone does not beat prior work}. Adding the deep fusion head brings $0.826$, a gain
of $0.072$ that is only marginal in a paired test ($p=0.075$). Replacing the read-out---same signal, same
encoder, no additional parameters devoted to fusion---brings $0.935$ ($p=3.3{\times}10^{-5}$ against the
fusion head).

The progression makes the paper's claim concrete. Each learned stage of the \emph{estimation} pipeline
buys something, but the single largest step, and the only one that is unambiguously significant, is the
one that changes \emph{how the decision is read out of the signal} rather than how the signal is
encoded. We draw the deployable conclusion accordingly: extraction, a compact encoder, and a
distributional read-out, with the fusion machinery omitted.

\subsection{The clinical task is classification, not regression}\label{sec:framing}
Sections~\ref{sec:sota}--\ref{sec:simple} operate within the prevailing paradigm---estimate a rate, then
threshold. Table~\ref{tab:framing} and Fig.~\ref{fig:isolation} show that abandoning it is the single largest gain in
this paper.
Reframing tachypnea as \emph{classification of the raw respiratory signal} rather than
rate-then-threshold regression is the paper's largest single lever, and its cleanest evidence is the
frozen-encoder objective comparison (Sec.~\ref{sec:objective}): on one \emph{fixed} representation,
replacing an L1-regression head with a classification objective raises AUROC from $0.833$ to $0.880$
(DeLong $p=0.026$) at equal or worse RR-MAE.
To rule out an unfair head-to-head, every published extractor is scored three equivalent ways in the
supplement---raw-RR ranking, RR-minus-threshold margin, and a discriminative read-out---and PRISM leads on
all three, so its advantage is not an artefact of giving PRISM alone a specialized classification head.
We realize this framing as a calibrated salience network (RespSalience, Sec.~\ref{sec:respsal}). Across
\emph{three seeds} on the matched corrected cohort it reaches AUROC $0.884{\pm}0.020$
versus $0.852$ for the regression-trained PRISM on the same
subjects: \emph{every} seed exceeds regression, though no single seed does so significantly at this
sample size, while a generic random-convolution classifier (ROCKET~\cite{rocket}, $0.915$
on the full split) lands in the same band. No single classifier is clearly best; the improvement is a
property of the decision \emph{framing}, not of any particular architecture---a transferable finding rather
than a fragile architecture claim, of which the same-encoder result above is the most controlled form.

The gain is also \emph{threshold-agnostic}, and we can say precisely when it is large. Sweeping the
decision cutoff across the observed range ($22$--$64$~breaths/min) gives a positive
classification-over-regression AUROC gain at \emph{every} cutoff for all three flow backbones
(EfficientPhys, TS-CAN, PRISM; $100\%$ of the $15$ non-degenerate thresholds, subject-bootstrapped), and
the advantage survives at the opposite, \emph{bradypnea} end: at a low-rate cutoff ($\le\!30$, prevalence
matched to the tachypnea base rate) classification again beats regression for every backbone (gain
$+0.028$ to $+0.072$; DeLong $p\le0.04$; Fig.~\ref{fig:framing-thr}). The improvement is thus a property of
the decision framing itself, not of the WHO tachypnea thresholds. Its \emph{magnitude} is not constant,
however: it grows monotonically with the cutoff, from $+0.03$--$0.09$ at low rates to $+0.26$--$0.37$ at
high rates. The mechanism is that rate regression is accurate where the extractor is (low rates, no
saturation: regression AUROC $0.88$--$0.93$) and fails where the extractor saturates (high rates:
regression AUROC $0.73$--$0.82$), so classification-on-signal contributes exactly where the regressed rate
is unreliable. Consistent with this, the two framings issue \emph{identical} decisions except within
$\sim\!2.5$~breaths/min of the cutoff (versus $\sim\!12$ for cases they agree on)---i.e.\ precisely where a
hard threshold on a noisy estimate is fragile. The framing gain is therefore best read as scaling with the
\emph{unreliability of the rate estimate}: largest for neonates, high rates, and degraded signals (the
NCHSDB degradation sweep shows the same trend), and near zero when the rate is easy to estimate. This also
explains why PRISM shows the smallest gain of the three---its fused rate is the strongest regression
baseline---reinforcing that the win is the classification \emph{head}, not the modalities.

\subsection{Isolating the objective from the representation}\label{sec:objective}
The comparisons above vary the model \emph{and} the objective together. To isolate the objective, we freeze
a single PRISM encoder and train nine prediction heads on the \emph{identical} fused features, subjects, and
five subject-disjoint folds, changing only the loss (Table~\ref{tab:objective}). The regression heads
(L1, Huber) minimize RR-MAE ($6.12$--$6.20$) but give the \emph{lowest} tachypnea AUROC ($0.833$--$0.842$);

focal) significantly improve discrimination---up to $0.880$ (BCE), $+0.047$ over L1 (DeLong $p=0.026$)---at
equal or \emph{worse} MAE. Because the representation is held fixed, this dissociation---a better clinical
decision without a better RR-MAE---attributes the gain to the training \emph{objective}, not the learned
representation. It is the paper's central claim in its most controlled form.

\textbf{The objective tracks respiration, not a confound.} A counterfactual probe confirms the decision head
operates on the breathing rhythm itself rather than an age, site, or motion correlate: time-scaling the
input waveform by $0.8$--$1.2\times$ moves the predicted rate and tachypnea probability in the expected
direction for \emph{every} clip, and notching out the respiratory-frequency peak collapses the prediction
($\Delta\bar p{=}0.085$), whereas phase-randomization that preserves the power spectrum (and hence the rate)
barely perturbs it ($\Delta\bar p{=}0.001$; details in the supplementary material).

\subsection{Age bands and the neonatal read-out}\label{sec:age}
Table~\ref{tab:headline} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ageband} break performance down by WHO band. Under the
regression-then-threshold rule the neonatal band collapses: sensitivity is $0.10$ for the reference
model and $0.30$ for the salience variants---at most three of ten tachypneic neonates detected. The
natural reading is that a camera cannot resolve neonatal breathing. That reading is wrong, and the
distinction decides whether the remedy is a sensor or a decision rule.

\textbf{The information survives acquisition.} A per-clip spectral probe over all $332$ cached flow
signals finds a detectable peak at the true rate in $100\%$ of cases with true RR $\ge\!60$ ($n{=}37$;
median $8.5\times$ the respiratory-band noise floor), including every one of the ten $<$2mo neonates at
true $60$--$80.5$~breaths/min. A plain FFT peak-pick on that same signal flags $7/10$ of those neonates
against the regression rule's $1/10$ (exact McNemar $p=0.031$; over all fast breathers $31/37$ vs.\
$18/37$, $p=2\times10^{-4}$, with all $13$ discordant pairs favouring the spectrum). The $0.15$--$1.5$~Hz
band edge is not responsible either: only one of the $37$ fast cases has its true rate above it.

\textbf{The failure is the decision rule, not the representation.} The model is \emph{not} beaten on
discrimination---tachypnea AUROC $0.853$ for the calibrated head against $0.828$ for the spectral-peak
margin, and $0.746$ against $0.718$ within $<$2mo. What fails is thresholding a regressed rate that has
been shrunk toward the training mean, where rates $\ge\!60$ are only $11\%$ of subjects: the model's
bias at true RR $\ge\!60$ is $-16.9$~breaths/min against the spectrum's $-3.7$, so the prediction
seldom crosses the WHO cutoff. Neonatal probabilities are compressed to match---the cut point selected
on calibration is $0.105{\pm}0.048$ for $<$2mo against $0.386{\pm}0.025$ for $2$--$12$mo---so no single
global threshold can serve both bands.

\textbf{Reading the distribution per age band recovers the neonate.} Holding the model, the scores and
the folds fixed and changing only the read-out---thresholding the calibrated tachypnea mass at a cut
point chosen \emph{per age band on out-of-fold calibration data alone}, never on the evaluated
fold---lifts neonatal sensitivity from $0.10$ to $0.70$ $[0.38,1.00]$ (Table~\ref{tab:readout}). Across
the cohort $15$ tachypneic subjects are recovered and \emph{none} are lost (McNemar $p=10^{-4}$), and the
same one-sided pattern holds within each band ($<$2mo $6$ recovered / $0$ lost, $p=0.031$; $2$--$12$mo
$8/0$, $p=0.008$). The distributional model is what makes this affordable rather than merely possible:
at matched $0.70$ neonatal sensitivity RespSalience holds specificity $0.929$ where the
regression model holds $0.607$, and overall it reaches sensitivity $0.690$ at specificity $0.956$
against the regression model's $0.702$ at $0.855$---nearly the same recall for a tenth of the false
alarms.

For continuity with earlier reports we also give the $\ge$2mo screen under the original rule
(sensitivity $0.79$ $[0.71,0.89]$, AUROC $0.912$ $[0.900,0.961]$; 12--60mo sensitivity $0.89$, AUROC
$0.947$). We report the $<$2mo band rather than omitting it: its AUROC is
$0.873$, i.e.\ the model \emph{ranks} neonates well, while its sensitivity at the deployed cut point is
only $0.30$ and its RR-MAE is $10.1$. That combination---good ranking, poor thresholded recall---is the
read-out gap in its clearest form, and it is why the band is escalated rather than declared
unscreenable. We no longer regard blanket neonatal exclusion as justified by our data: the neonate is
screenable by this camera pipeline once the decision is read from the distribution rather than from a
thresholded point estimate. The residual cost is specificity in a small band ($n{=}38$, $10$
tachypneic), so we retain neonatal escalation as a \emph{safety} policy below rather than as a
statement about what the sensor can see.

Neonatal escalation is therefore a conservative policy choice rather than a concession that the band is
unscreenable, and we keep it because the recovered neonatal operating point trades specificity in the
smallest and most fragile band ($n{=}38$). We operationalize it not as a blanket exclusion but as an
explicit, uncertainty-driven \emph{escalation} pathway. Under the age-conditional conformal rule
(Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}), whose leakage-free interval widens to $\pm19$~breaths/min for neonates against
$\pm7$ for the oldest band, the selective triage refers a case to escalation (radar or a contact
measurement) whenever its prediction interval straddles the WHO threshold. This routes cases out in
proportion to how uncertain the regressed rate is, which orders by age---the referral rate is $53\%$ for
$<$2mo, $46\%$ for $2$--$12$mo, and $44\%$ for $12$--$60$mo (Fig.~\ref{fig:escalation})---though the
gradient across bands is modest. That $53\%$ is what age-conditional conformal triage refers on its own;
because neonates are \emph{additionally} placed on mandatory referral by policy, their effective referral
rate is $100\%$ and the triage is never used to \emph{clear} a neonate. On the retained (non-referred)
cases the screen is clean---specificity $0.98$ and sensitivity $1.00$ for the oldest band, $0.69$
pooled---so escalation buys a real safety margin for a $45\%$ coverage cost. Concretely, of the ten
tachypneic neonates (true RR $60$--$80.5$) that a camera-only screen almost entirely clears ($1/10$
detected), age-conditional escalation refers seven to a modality that can see them, cutting neonatal
false-clears from nine to three. The residual three are the most saturated cases (predicted $29$--$39$ for
true $66$--$78$), where even a $\pm19$~breaths/min interval leaves the upper bound at $48$--$58$, below the
$60$ cutoff: escalation \emph{mitigates} the neonatal blind spot but does not eliminate it. We stress that
this behavior is a property of the \emph{age-conditional} calibration; a pooled conformal width fitted the
same way covers neonates at only $0.579$ against a nominal $0.80$, inverts the gradient by escalating the
youngest band least ($16\%$) and the oldest most ($59\%$), and rescues two rather than six---precisely the
failure the age-conditional rule corrects.

\emph{A note on the triage layer.} The escalation rule above forms a conformal interval around the
\emph{regressed} rate and refers whenever it straddles the cutoff---so the layer deciding whom to screen
is built on exactly the read-out this paper argues against, and inherits its failure mode. Replacing it
with abstention on the salience mass, at matched leakage discipline on the same cohort, lowers the total
downstream burden (REFER${+}$ALERT) from $52.4\%$ to $44.3\%$ (exact McNemar $p=0.006$) while the number
of tachypneic children auto-cleared falls from nine to one. We claim only the burden reduction: split
into two independent three-seed ensembles the burden effect reaches significance in one half and the
safety effect in neither, so mandatory neonatal referral stays. The comparison, the per-band table and
the split analysis are in the supplementary material.

\emph{A note on model selection.} Selecting checkpoints by validation regression error rather than by
the clinical endpoint measurably changes that endpoint, and the published extractors we compare against
were never checkpoint-selected at all---their drivers evaluate whatever weights remain resident when
training returns. We therefore gave every model the same treatment before comparing, and report the full
selection-rule $\times$ model-family matrix in the supplementary material; it is the reason the
head-to-head in Table~\ref{tab:main} is a fair one.

\subsection{An external replication that fails, and what it localises}\label{sec:extval}
The framing claim and the neonatal recovery are established on a single video cohort. We attempted an
independent replication on the NCH Sleep DataBank (NCHSDB), a pediatric polysomnography corpus, and
report it here in full because it did not succeed. An earlier version of this manuscript reported a
decisive confirmation on this corpus; neither the analysis code nor its outputs survived, we have
rebuilt the pipeline from the released recordings, and the result does not reproduce. We therefore
withdraw that claim.

\textbf{Cohort and design.} We use the $70$ youngest available studies ($0.2$--$1.8$ months;
\texttt{scripts/nchsdb\_select\_records.py}), yielding $1{,}645$ non-overlapping $60$-s windows after
quality control, of which $234$ ($14.2\%$) exceed the WHO neonatal threshold of $60$~breaths/min,
spread across $41$ of the $70$ infants. Unlike our video cohorts this corpus \emph{contains} genuine
neonatal fast breathing. The design is non-circular: the \emph{input} is the thoracic belt (chest-wall
motion, the analogue a camera observes) and the \emph{label} is derived from airflow---a thermistor and
a nasal-pressure transducer that must agree within $3$~breaths/min for a window to be admitted---with
the inductance belts excluded, so the input never informs its own label. We had previously described
this label as airflow \emph{and capnography}; the end-tidal CO$_2$ channel carries a corrupt digital
calibration range in a majority of records and yields no usable rate, so the reference is airflow-only.

\textbf{Result.} Two independent implementations agree that there is no framing advantage here. Using
the head-swap of Section~\ref{sec:framing}---the signal's own spectral rate thresholded at the cutoff
versus a ROCKET classifier on the same waveform, deterministic and subject-grouped---the difference is
null at every degradation level ($\Delta$AUROC ${+}0.014$, ${+}0.026$, ${-}0.056$, ${-}0.014$ as
additive noise rises; every interval spans zero, $P(\text{gain}{>}0)=0.25$--$0.62$). Replacing both
read-outs with trained heads on a shared encoder, averaged over five seeds, gives the same answer:
every difference is negative but none is significant (${-}0.032$, ${-}0.027$, ${-}0.074$, ${-}0.005$ as
noise rises; $p=0.13$--$0.93$). We note that single runs of the learned comparison were unusable: at fixed nominal
seed, repeated runs produced deltas of ${-}0.062$ and ${+}0.108$ for the same configuration, so any
conclusion drawn from one training run of this size would have been an artefact.

\textbf{What this localises.} The null is informative rather than merely disappointing, because the
condition our own account requires is absent here. We argue that the framing advantage arises when a
\emph{trained rate estimator shrinks its extreme predictions toward the training mean}, so that
thresholding the point estimate discards cases the ranking still separates. On a contact belt no such
shrinkage occurs: the rate is directly recoverable, and an untrained spectral peak on the raw belt
already reaches AUROC $0.746$ for neonatal tachypnea. Where the estimate is accurate, reading the
distribution cannot help, and our results say exactly that. Adding noise does not restore the effect,
because generic noise degrades both read-outs symmetrically---it does not induce the asymmetric
shrinkage that a trained regressor exhibits on a saturating sensor.

\textbf{Testing that account directly.} The preceding paragraph is an explanation of a null, which is
cheap to assert and easy to fit after the fact. It also makes a falsifiable prediction: if the
advantage requires a front end that cannot represent fast rates, then \emph{inducing} that condition on
this same belt should make the advantage appear, and doing so gradually should make it appear
gradually. We tested this by low-passing the belt signal before training, holding the cohort, the
encoder, the folds and both read-outs fixed, and sweeping the cutoff from no filtering down to
$0.7$~Hz---through $1.0$~Hz, which is the $60$~breaths/min threshold itself. Saturation is measured on
the output, where the mechanism acts: $\sigma = 1 - $ the slope of predicted rate on true rate, so
$\sigma=0$ is a faithful estimator and $\sigma\to1$ one that ignores the true rate.

The prediction holds (Fig.~\ref{fig:saturation}). As the cutoff falls, $\sigma$ rises from $0.615$ to
$0.705$ and the framing gain rises monotonically with it, from ${-}0.042$ to ${+}0.024$: Spearman
$\rho={+}0.94$ against $\sigma$ ($p=0.005$), $\rho={+}0.89$ against the manipulation itself ($p=0.019$),
and a seed-weighted least-squares slope of ${+}0.78$~AUROC per unit $\sigma$ ($p=0.001$). The gain
crosses zero between the $1.1$ and $0.9$~Hz cutoffs---that is, at the point where the pass-band stops
admitting $60$~breaths/min and a fast breather can no longer be represented. The effect can therefore
be switched on and off at will, which is the strongest evidence in this paper that the advantage is
mechanistic rather than incidental to one cohort.

We state three limits, because the design is modest. The swing is $0.066$~AUROC, an order of magnitude
smaller than the ${+}0.14$ to ${+}0.34$ observed on camera front ends, so this establishes the direction
of the relationship and not its magnitude. Six conditions drawn from one corpus are not six independent
samples, so these $p$-values describe an ordering that is unlikely to arise by chance rather than an
effect sampled from a population; the test against the manipulation ($p=0.019$) is the one that does
not partly presuppose its own predictor, and is the one we rely on. And $\sigma$ is computed
window-level here but subject-level for the camera extractors, so the two are not on a common scale and
we do not plot or model them together.

The honest conclusion is that we have external evidence for the \emph{scope} of the claim rather than
for the claim itself. The framing advantage is a property of decisions formed on a degraded,
saturating front end---which is what our video cohort provides and what a contact belt does not---and
we can no longer assert that it transfers to any sensor. A genuine external test requires a second
cohort with camera-derived respiration and real tachypnea; the public infant video sets contain no
fast-breathing cases (RR $12$--$38$), so no such test is currently available to us. That absence is
now the single most important limitation of this work.

\subsection{Robustness, calibration, and deployability}\label{sec:calib}
\emph{Extractor robustness, and a retraction.} The clinical result does not depend on the flow backbone:
a TS-CAN-derived flow reaches AUROC $0.897$ under a leakage-free per-fold protocol, comparable to PRISM.
We flag and \textbf{retract} an apparent TS-CAN improvement (AUROC $0.911$) reported in an earlier
version, which we traced to extractor leakage across folds. Re-auditing our own pipeline the same way
moves the headline the \emph{wrong} way for a leakage explanation---a per-fold held-out EfficientPhys
extractor gives $0.855$ against the full-split $0.826$, i.e.\ $0.029$ higher, and not significantly
different either way (DeLong $p=0.45$)---so extractor leakage cannot be what produced our result. The
full audit is in the supplementary material. Trained on Pediatric-RR and evaluated out of domain on
AIR-400, the model attains RR-MAE $3.12$, competitive with the infant-specific state of the
art~\cite{infant_air}, while hand-crafted optical flow collapses ($11.3$--$11.8$); that contrast is what
justifies the learned flow channel (Table~\ref{tab:crossdata}).

\emph{Calibration and operating points.} The model is well calibrated (ECE $0.064$, Brier $0.110$) and
its net benefit exceeds both treat-all and treat-none at every threshold on decision-curve analysis
(Fig.~\ref{fig:calib}). The age-conditional conformal layer attains nominal coverage when calibrated
leave-one-fold-out ($0.81$ overall against a $0.80$ target, and $0.83/0.81/0.81$ across the three bands);
this is a property of the age-conditional construction rather than of conformal prediction generally, and
a single pooled width under-covers exactly where it matters most---$0.62$ for neonates (supplementary
material). For a screen the decisive operating point fixes \emph{sensitivity}, and this is where the
advantage is largest: at sensitivity $0.90$ the model retains specificity $0.65$ against $0.05$--$0.21$
for the five published extractors, a several-fold reduction in false referrals at the sensitivity a
screen must actually reach. At a fixed specificity of $0.90$ it retains sensitivity $0.70$. Positive
predictive value is $0.73$ on this cohort (prevalence $0.26$) but falls to $0.46$ at a community
prevalence of $0.10$, while negative predictive value stays high ($0.96$): the screen rules out better
than it rules in, which is the appropriate behaviour for triage but must be stated plainly for anyone
deploying it. We find no significant sex disparity, and on the cohort's own clinical sign (observed
difficulty breathing) neither true nor estimated respiratory rate is more than weakly predictive
(AUROC $0.59$ and $0.54$)---an honest reminder that tachypnea is a screening sign, not a disease label.
Both analyses are reported in full in the supplementary material.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}
\textbf{Metric reframing.} Our central message is methodological: a screen should be judged by its
clinical discrimination and calibration, not by RR-MAE. The claim needs stating carefully, because our
own data bound it. Among the five published extractors---all trained to regress the rate---RR-MAE ranks
clinical utility well ($\rho=-0.90$, $p=0.037$); a reviewer is right to expect that within a method
family, better regression means a better screen. The dissociation appears when the objective changes:
four of the five are better regressors than PRISM yet all five are worse screens
(Section~\ref{sec:sota}), and the same reversal recurs when we hold the representation fixed and swap
only the objective, the read-out, or the checkpoint-selection criterion. RR-MAE is therefore not a
useless metric but an \emph{intra-family} one, and it cannot arbitrate between methods that form the
clinical decision differently. The non-significant McNemar result sharpens rather than
weakens this point: AUROC captures the separability the extractors lack, while the fixed-threshold
decision is dominated by the shared difficulty of the cohort near the boundary; the practical implication
is that deployment should tune the operating point (e.g., to a target sensitivity) and re-estimate the
decision-level benefit prospectively.

\textbf{What actually matters.} A component isolation (Section~\ref{sec:simple}) locates the clinical
performance precisely. It is \emph{not} the raw extraction: the learned flow, read out by FFT, discriminates
tachypnea at only $0.72$, no better than an end-to-end video CNN. It is \emph{not} the deep fusion head: a
calibrated linear model on the encoder's per-modality outputs is statistically indistinguishable from the
full network. It \emph{is} the compact, self-supervised spectral-temporal encoder, which lifts the raw
signal from $0.72$ to $0.87$ ($p<10^{-4}$) and is exactly what the ablation flags as load-bearing. For a
low-resource setting this is an actionable design rule: invest in the per-modality encoder and pretraining,
keep the readout linear and calibratable, and do not add fusion machinery that a logistic regression
already subsumes.

\textbf{Clinical translation.} A sensitivity of $0.71$ for the $\ge$2mo screen is not a stand-alone
diagnosis; it is a triage signal that, combined with mandatory neonatal referral and calibrated
abstention, can prioritize scarce clinician attention. Calibration and positive net benefit
(Section~\ref{sec:calib}) are what make such a signal safe to act on.

\textbf{The neonatal limit is a read-out limit.} The apparent RGB saturation near $35$~breaths/min
affects every extractor we tested, and we first reported it as partly an acquisition limit. A direct
test of that claim does not support it. The raw respiratory-band SNR does fall with rate, but not to
the point of erasure: the true rate remains a detectable spectral peak in $100\%$ of fast-breathing
clips, and a plain FFT peak-pick recovers most of the neonatal tachypnea the trained regression rule
misses (Section~\ref{sec:age}). High-rate information is attenuated, not lost, and we withdraw the
suggestion that a higher-fidelity front end is the necessary remedy.

What actually fails is the last step. A regressor trained where fast rates are rare shrinks its
extreme predictions toward the mean, and thresholding that shrunken estimate is what erases the
neonate; the model's own ranking of these cases is nearly unimpaired. Reading the calibrated
distribution at an age-specific operating point recovers neonatal sensitivity from $0.10$ to $0.70$
without touching the sensor, the extractor or the weights. This is the sharpest evidence in the paper
for its central thesis: the decisive choice is how the clinical decision is formed, not how accurately
the rate is regressed. It also revises our own earlier framing---we previously treated the neonatal
band as evidence for a sensing gap, and it is better understood as evidence for a modelling one.

Radar remains attractive for the neonate, but its role changes. It is no longer the only route past a
physical ceiling; it is a complementary modality whose value is now an empirical question about
specificity in the band where camera specificity is weakest ($0.61$--$0.79$ at matched sensitivity).
Our radar and RGB validations remain on different cohorts, so an integrated RGB$+$radar study on the
same infants---especially real neonates in cribs---is still the natural next step. For the record, our
radar measurements on cohorts separate from ours are exact on a thorax phantom across
$20$--$80$~breaths/min (MAE $0.00$, $r{=}1.00$) and clinical-grade on $50$ real children (MAE $1.50$,
$r{=}0.97$, $94\%$ within $3$~breaths/min). We deliberately do not report these as a result of this
paper: radar was motivated by a neonatal ceiling we now attribute to the read-out rather than to the
sensor, and an unintegrated modality validated on other cohorts cannot support a claim about this
screen.

\textbf{Comparison to published numbers.} Infant-specific methods report $\approx$2.9~RR-MAE in
domain~\cite{infant_air}; we reproduce comparable MAE ($2.93$ on AIR-400) but show that such MAE does not
by itself deliver tachypnea discrimination on a clinical cohort, which is the quantity a screen needs.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i) The clinical cohort spans three Bangladeshi sites (Institute of Child and Mother
Health, an Upazila Health Complex, and a Community Clinic; per-site tachypnea AUROC $0.80$--$0.94$,
supplementary). Its reference standard is a multi-reader VEP consensus rather than a single bedside
count, which is stronger than we had previously described it, but it remains a \emph{visual} reference
derived from the same chest-motion signal the model observes; an independent physiological reference
(capnography, airflow, or respiratory-inductance plethysmography) is still needed. Two residual
concerns follow from the panel procedure itself. Videos for which no reader pair agreed within
$2$~breaths/min were excluded from the release, so this benchmark is cleaner than the full clinical
population and our operating points may be optimistic relative to unselected practice. And the
consensus, while reproducible, is not independent of the modality under test. We previously described the
independent replication (Sec.~\ref{sec:extval}) as capnography-referenced; on inspection of the released
recordings that is not correct, and we withdraw it. The end-tidal CO$_2$ channel carries a corrupt
digital calibration range in a majority of the records we examined ($8$ of $14$), which decodes to a
signal pinned at its physical maximum and yields no usable rate. The replication's reference is
therefore \emph{airflow} only---derived from two independent airflow transducers (thermistor and nasal
pressure) that must agree to within $3$~breaths/min for a window to be admitted---and no
capnography-referenced evidence is available to us. In particular, the
classification-framing gain and the neonatal recovery are demonstrated on the only available tachypnea
cohort; the external infant sets (AIR-125/400) contain \emph{no} fast-breathing cases (RR $12$--$38$,
all age $6$~months) and so cannot test them. The neonatal recovery in particular rests on $38$ subjects of whom $10$ are tachypneic, and it is worth
separating what that does and does not support. The \emph{comparative} claim is a paired one and is
adequately powered: $6$ neonates recovered against $0$ lost (exact McNemar $p=0.031$), with no
tachypneic subject lost in any band cohort-wide ($15$ recovered, $0$ lost, $p=10^{-4}$), and the
recovered sensitivity is identically $0.70$ under all three models we tested. The \emph{absolute}
neonatal operating point is not: at $7/10$ the exact interval is $[0.35,0.93]$, a half-width of $0.29$.
Two things follow. First, we report the paired contrast as the result and treat the neonatal
sensitivity value as an effect size, not a specification. Second, the imprecision is quantifiable
rather than open-ended: reaching a half-width of $0.15$ requires roughly $40$ tachypneic neonates
($\approx\!150$ neonates screened at this cohort's $26\%$ neonatal tachypnea rate), and $0.10$ requires
roughly $88$ ($\approx\!335$ screened). That is the concrete target a confirmatory study should be
powered to, and it is well within a single-site prospective enrolment.

Partial pooling reduces, but does not remove, the exposure. Shrinking each band's cut point toward the
pooled one in proportion to the positives it carries cuts the fold-to-fold standard deviation of the
neonatal threshold by roughly a third ($0.048\rightarrow0.031$) and raises overall specificity
($0.855\rightarrow0.891$) at unchanged overall sensitivity, at the cost of one neonatal detection
($0.70\rightarrow0.60$ with specificity $0.61\rightarrow0.75$). We report the unshrunk rule as the
primary result because it is the more conservative choice for a screen, and the shrunk rule as the
variant we would deploy where false referrals are expensive. We surveyed the available public alternatives and found them inadequate for a
fair test: respiratory-sound sets (ICBHI~\cite{icbhi_rocha}) yield only weakly recoverable respiration from lung-sound audio,
a preliminary probe on preterm-waveform data (PICS~\cite{pics}, 9 infants) is \emph{consistent} with the
gain---classification AUROC $0.96$ vs.\ regression $0.78$ on impedance windows---but has too few subjects
to be rigorous; and the one large pediatric resource (the NCH Sleep DataBank~\cite{nchsdb}, $3{,}673$
children) is credentialed and captures sleep rather than acute tachypnea. A prospective, clean-signal pediatric cohort
\emph{with} tachypnea is thus the single most important remaining validation.
(ii) Skin-tone equity is not auditable in a single-population cohort, a documented risk for optical
methods~\cite{nowara_skintone}. (iii) Tachypnea is a weak proxy for disease (AUROC $0.59$ for difficulty
breathing). (iv) The radar arm is validated on a phantom and on real children, but not yet on real
neonates. (v)~We had attributed the non-significant McNemar results to inter-rater error in manual breath
counting. Having established that the reference is a multi-reader consensus rather than a single count,
we withdraw that explanation: at the panel's measured reader error only about seven labels in the cohort
are unstable, too few to account for it (Sec.~\ref{sec:ceiling}). Separately, a small number ($15$) of validation signals in an earlier
pipeline used a hand-crafted extractor, affecting only checkpoint selection---all reported \emph{test}
metrics use the learned extractor on held-out folds. These define a prospective, outcome-anchored,
multi-modal study.

\section{Conclusion}
In non-contact respiratory screening the decision rule dominates the architecture. We show this at four
points in one pipeline, each holding the representation fixed: the training objective, the read-out
head, the age-conditional operating point, and the criterion used to select a checkpoint. Every one of
them moves the clinical endpoint; swapping the sequence backbone does not. The model we end up
recommending is accordingly the simplest one in the paper---a single channel, no fusion, no auxiliary
components---and it is also the best, dominating the multimodal regression baseline in every age band on
both the clinical endpoint and RR-MAE.

The sharpest consequence is the neonatal band. What we and others had read as a limit of what a camera
can see is a limit of how the decision is read out: the true rate is present in the extracted signal,
the model ranks it well, and an age-specific threshold on the calibrated distribution recovers it.
Radar remains a promising complementary route to the neonate, but it is no longer required to make that
band screenable.

We are explicit about what we could not establish. Our attempt to replicate the framing effect on an
independent neonatal cohort failed, and the failure is informative: on a contact belt the rate is
directly recoverable, so there is no regression shrinkage for a distributional read-out to repair. The
claim is therefore bounded to decisions formed on degraded, saturating front ends, and a genuine
external test awaits a second cohort with camera-derived respiration and real tachypnea, which no
public resource currently provides.

We advocate that non-contact respiration work report the clinical endpoint, calibration, and
age-stratified performance rather than RR-MAE alone; that model selection be treated as part of the
decision rule and reported as such; and that an apparent sensing ceiling be tested against the read-out
before it is accepted as physical.

\section*{Reproducibility}
All metrics are computed from per-fold prediction files with subject-disjoint splits; DeLong and McNemar
use a paired implementation on matched subjects. Age-band, calibration, and decision-curve numbers derive
from the same prediction files.

Every model reported in this paper is trained deterministically, and we describe why that is worth
saying, because seeding alone does not achieve it. Fixing the Python, NumPy, and Torch seeds does
\emph{not} make GPU training reproducible: cuDNN's convolution backward accumulates with atomics whose
summation order varies between launches, so repeated runs of identical code at an identical seed
diverge. In an earlier, non-deterministic version of this work three replicates of the same protocol
gave ensemble AUROCs of $0.936$, $0.949$, and $0.945$ and a per-seed spread of up to $0.046$, with
individual subject probabilities differing by as much as $0.65$ between runs. That spread is small
against the $+0.100$ gap this paper reports, but it is \emph{larger} than several component-level
effects, and it invalidated one of them: an apparent benefit from self-supervised pretraining
($p=0.044$) proved to be a single favourable draw and was removed from the model
(Sec.~\ref{sec:framing}).

We therefore pin the non-deterministic kernels (\texttt{cudnn.deterministic}, no autotuning,
deterministic algorithm selection, and a fixed cuBLAS workspace), and training now reproduces
bit-identically: two independent runs at the same seed agree to $0$ on every one of the $332$ subject
predictions. Every number in this paper comes from that setting and can be regenerated exactly, and we
report a six-seed rather than a three-seed ensemble so that no result depends on which seeds were drawn.
The practical lesson generalises beyond this cohort: on a dataset of this size, an ablation evaluated
from a single training run cannot distinguish a real component from run-to-run noise, and should not be
reported as if it could.
