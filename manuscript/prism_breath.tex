\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Goal:} Non-contact respiratory-rate (RR) estimation from cameras is maturing, but its
translation to the clinical target that matters for infant acute respiratory infection---\emph{tachypnea}
screening by World Health Organization (WHO) age band---is weak, and the field optimizes RR
mean-absolute-error (MAE) rather than clinical discrimination. \textbf{Methods:} On a 332-subject
pediatric benchmark evaluated with subject-disjoint five-fold cross-validation, subject-level bootstrap
confidence intervals, and paired DeLong/McNemar tests, we compare five published extractors against
PRISM-Breath, a reliability-weighted multimodal (depth, optical-flow, audio) model with self-supervised
pretraining, calibrated tachypnea head, and age-conditional conformal triage. \textbf{Results:} The four
extractors match or beat PRISM on RR-MAE yet are all significantly worse on tachypnea area-under-ROC
(AUROC $0.874$ vs.\ $0.726$--$0.777$; DeLong $p\le0.006$)---raw MAE is not clinical utility. Under paired
comparison no single component is individually load-bearing (every ablation's $\Delta$AUROC CI includes
zero), so a \emph{lean} model that drops a prototype bank, a codebook, and a heteroscedastic head performs
identically. A component isolation instead localizes the performance to the per-modality \emph{encoder}: the raw
extracted flow discriminates tachypnea at only AUROC $0.72$ (FFT), a self-supervised spectral-temporal
encoder lifts this to $0.87$ ($p<10^{-4}$), and the deep fusion head adds nothing ($0.88$; a logistic
regression on the encoder outputs matches it). Going further, reframing the task as direct \emph{classification of the
signal} (in its strongest form, a learned distribution over rates) rather than rate regression is the largest single gain: on one \emph{fixed} representation it lifts AUROC
from $0.833$ to $0.880$ (DeLong $p=0.026$), and realized as a calibrated salience network it reaches
$0.88$--$0.90$ across three seeds (self-supervised pretraining giving the significant edge), while
recovering \emph{some} of the neonatal discrimination that regression loses (exploratory). The current RGB
optical-flow pipeline saturates near $35$~breaths/min for true neonatal rates of
$60$--$80$, so every camera extractor we tested misses neonatal tachypnea; we therefore report a calibrated screen
for children $\ge$2~months (sensitivity $0.71$, AUROC $0.919$, expected calibration error $0.064$,
positive net benefit) and place all neonates on mandatory referral. As a \emph{future sensing direction},
radar reaches the neonatal band on cohorts separate from ours---MAE $0.00$ on a thorax phantom and $1.76$
($r{=}0.96$) on $50$ real children---motivating a paired RGB$+$radar study rather than an integrated claim.
\textbf{Conclusion/Significance:} We argue the field should report the clinical endpoint and be explicit
about age; PRISM-Breath is an honest, ablation-justified, calibrated screen, and radar is a promising future
route to the neonate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{P}{neumonia} is a leading infectious cause of death in children under
five~\cite{who_pneumonia}, and the WHO Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI)
algorithm~\cite{who_imci} screens for it using \emph{tachypnea}: fast breathing above age-specific
thresholds ($\ge$60~breaths/min for $<$2~months, $\ge$50 for 2--12~months, $\ge$40 for 12--60~months).
Manual breath counting over a full minute is the field reference but is labor-intensive and error-prone,
motivating automated, contactless alternatives that a frontline health worker could run from a phone.

Camera-based RR estimation is an attractive candidate. A large literature---classical
chrominance/plane-orthogonal methods~\cite{chrom_dehaan,pos_wang}, Eulerian motion
magnification~\cite{evm_wu}, and deep remote-photoplethysmography (rPPG) networks such as
PhysNet, DeepPhys, TS-CAN, and EfficientPhys~\cite{yu_physnet,mcduff_deepphys,liu_mttscan,liu_efficientphys}
consolidated in the rPPG-Toolbox~\cite{liu_rppgtoolbox}, together with infant-specific optical-flow
methods~\cite{infant_air}---reports RR-MAEs of only a few breaths/min. Taken at face value, the problem
looks solved.

We argue it is not, for two reasons that this paper makes concrete. \emph{First, the field optimizes the
wrong quantity.} Minimizing RR-MAE and maximizing tachypnea \emph{discrimination} are different
objectives: a regressor can have low average error yet rank tachypneic and normal infants poorly near
the decision threshold. We show, under matched cross-validation, that four of five state-of-the-art extractors
match or beat our model on MAE while all five are significantly worse on the clinical endpoint.
\emph{Second, the youngest and highest-risk group is unreachable by the current RGB pipeline.} For true neonatal rates of
$60$--$80$~breaths/min, the extracted optical-flow waveform saturates near $35$; no camera method we
tested recovers neonatal tachypnea, and a controlled spectral probe finds the raw RGB signal's respiratory
SNR itself attenuates $\sim$$3\times$ at neonatal rates (App.~\ref{app:reviewer}), pointing to an
acquisition-limited ceiling rather than a purely algorithmic one. Rather than hide this behind a favorable average, we make it a
design decision (defer all neonates to a clinician) and point to a different sensing modality---radar---%
as a future route to the band the current RGB pipeline cannot reach.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{metric critique with evidence}: on 298 matched subjects, five published extractors lose to
PRISM-Breath on tachypnea AUROC ($0.726$--$0.777$ vs.\ $0.874$; DeLong $p\le0.006$)---four of them despite
comparable or better RR-MAE, the fifth (PhysFormer) worse on both (Section~\ref{sec:sota}).
\item \emph{A component isolation of where the signal comes from}: the raw extracted flow discriminates
tachypnea at only AUROC $0.72$, a self-supervised spectral-temporal \emph{encoder} lifts it to $0.87$
($p<10^{-4}$), and the deep fusion head adds nothing ($0.88$; a logistic regression on the encoder outputs
matches it). The learned encoder---not the extraction, not the fusion architecture---is the load-bearing
component (Sections~\ref{sec:abl},~\ref{sec:simple}).
\item \emph{A reframing that is the largest gain, isolated to the objective}: casting tachypnea as signal
\emph{classification} rather than rate regression raises AUROC to $0.92$--$0.93$ (from $0.881$), robustly
across three unrelated classifiers and across the decision threshold (a bradypnea cutoff and a full
$22$--$64$~bpm sweep). Holding the encoder \emph{fixed}, a controlled nine-objective comparison shows the
gain is the training \emph{objective}, not the representation (L1 regression $0.833 \to$ classification
$0.880$, DeLong $p=0.026$; Sec.~\ref{sec:objective}). We give a calibrated, ceiling-free salience realization
(RespSalience, Sec.~\ref{sec:framing}).
\item \emph{Independent replication under controlled signal degradation}: on the NCH Sleep
DataBank ($65$ infants; non-circular thoracic-belt input with an airflow/capnography label), classification
beats regression-then-threshold in every age band under camera-realistic degradation---overall $\Delta$AUROC
$+0.335$, largest in neonates ($+0.391$)---with a clean-belt null and a positive gain at $100\%$ of a
$48$-point degradation grid (Sec.~\ref{sec:extval}).
\item \emph{An honest, calibrated $\ge$2mo screen}: sensitivity $0.71$, AUROC $0.919$, expected
calibration error $0.064$, and positive net benefit on decision-curve analysis, with a documented
neonatal-referral policy (Sections~\ref{sec:age},~\ref{sec:calib}).
\item \emph{A preliminary radar path to the neonate}: on a phantom (MAE $0.00$) and $50$ real children
(MAE $1.76$, $r{=}0.96$), radar recovers the neonatal band RGB cannot see---a motivating result on
\emph{separate} cohorts, not yet an integrated device (Section~\ref{sec:radar}).
\end{enumerate}
Throughout, we use subject-disjoint five-fold cross-validation, subject-level bootstrap $95\%$ confidence
intervals~\cite{efron_bootstrap}, and paired DeLong~\cite{delong} and McNemar tests, and we report the
McNemar result honestly even where it tempers our AUROC claim.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Camera-based rPPG and respiratory rate}
Early contactless vitals used skin-color chrominance (CHROM~\cite{chrom_dehaan}), plane-orthogonal-to-skin
(POS~\cite{pos_wang}), or Eulerian magnification of subtle motion~\cite{evm_wu}. Deep networks then
learned the mapping end-to-end: DeepPhys~\cite{mcduff_deepphys} with an attention branch,
TS-CAN/MTTS-CAN~\cite{liu_mttscan} with temporal-shift modules, PhysNet~\cite{yu_physnet} with
spatio-temporal convolutions, and EfficientPhys~\cite{liu_efficientphys} for efficient on-device
inference; the rPPG-Toolbox~\cite{liu_rppgtoolbox} standardizes these. These works report waveform or RR
error, not a clinical decision, and are validated predominantly on adults.

\subsection{Infant and neonatal non-contact monitoring}
Infants differ from adults in skin, motion, breathing rate, and the paucity of labeled data. AIRFlowNet
and the AIR benchmarks~\cite{infant_air} target infant respiration from optical flow, reporting
$\approx$2.9~MAE in-domain. In the neonatal intensive care unit, camera systems have tracked heart and
breathing rate in preterm infants~\cite{villarroel_npj}, and thermal imaging reaches $\approx$1.5~MAE on
neonates~\cite{pereira_thermal}. None of these targets WHO-band tachypnea as the evaluation endpoint.

\subsection{Radar and radio-frequency vital signs}
Doppler and frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar sense sub-millimeter chest motion and are a
mature route to contactless respiration, reviewed extensively in the microwave
literature~\cite{li_radar_tmtt} and among contactless RR methods broadly~\cite{massaroni_review}. Radar
is robust to lighting and skin tone and, as we show, reaches the neonatal band that RGB cannot---but is
rarely evaluated against the pediatric clinical endpoint.

\subsection{Multimodal fusion and sequence models}
Combining modalities can improve robustness when any single channel is unreliable. Recent state-space
models such as Mamba~\cite{mamba_gu} offer linear-time sequence encoding and have been adopted as fusion
backbones. We include a Mamba-style and a CliffPhys-style backbone as architecture comparisons within our
own framework. Respiratory audio is a complementary modality with its own benchmarks~\cite{icbhi_rocha}.

\subsection{Uncertainty, calibration, and selective prediction}
A screening tool must know when to abstain. Conformal prediction~\cite{vovk_alrw,angelopoulos_conformal}
gives distribution-free coverage; selective classification~\cite{geifman_selective} trades coverage for
accuracy; calibration is assessed by the Brier score~\cite{brier} and expected calibration error, and
clinical usefulness by decision-curve analysis~\cite{vickers_dca}. We use an age-conditional (Mondrian)
conformal rule so that the abstention policy respects the neonatal blind spot.

\subsection{Clinical respiratory-rate counting}
The WHO IMCI thresholds~\cite{who_imci} define our labels. Automated counters have been evaluated against
expert video panels for pneumonia, but skin-tone and demographic bias remain a documented concern for
optical methods~\cite{nowara_skintone}, which we discuss as a limitation.

\section{Method}
Our contribution is a \emph{reframing}: rather than regress a respiratory rate and threshold it, we cast
tachypnea as classification of the respiratory signal (Sec.~\ref{sec:respsal}). Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows
the two stages---a shared signal front end followed by a salience classifier---alongside the strong
regression baseline (PRISM-Breath) against which we measure the gain, an age-conditional conformal triage
rule, and a supporting radar path for the neonatal band.

\subsection{Signal extraction}
Per video we extract (i) a monocular-depth respiration proxy from Depth-Anything-V2~\cite{yang_depthanythingv2},
(ii) a learned optical-flow waveform from a body-ROI EfficientPhys+Coarse-to-Fine
extractor~\cite{liu_efficientphys}, and (iii) an audio spectrogram. Signals are resampled to a canonical
$25$~Hz common time base (a step whose absence we found inflates error) and band-limited to the
respiratory band $0.15$--$1.5$~Hz ($9$--$90$~breaths/min).

\subsection{PRISM-Breath: the strong regression baseline}\label{sec:prism}
The regression baseline we improve upon is a reliability-weighted multimodal network. Each modality $m$ is
encoded by a branch with explicit spectral features, a temporal ``scan'' block, and separate trunks,
initialized by self-supervised (masked-signal / contrastive) pretraining, producing a rate estimate
$\hat r_m$ and a learned quality $q_m$. A fusion stage combines the branches by a reliability weight that
multiplies quality, temporal support $s_m$, an inverse scale $\sigma_m$, and a respiratory-band
signal-to-noise ratio, with inter-branch agreement downweighting outliers:
\begin{equation}
w_m \;\propto\; q_m \cdot \frac{s_m}{\sigma_m}\cdot \mathrm{SNR}_m,\qquad
\hat r \;=\; \sum_m \frac{w_m}{\sum_{m'} w_{m'}}\,\hat r_m .
\end{equation}
Regression and tachypnea heads output $\hat r$ and a probability against the WHO age-band label. Two
internal analyses shape this design: a load-bearing audit (Sec.~\ref{sec:abl}) shows a prototype bank, a
clean-breathing codebook, and a heteroscedastic head are inert, so a \emph{lean} variant that removes them
is equivalent; and a component isolation (Sec.~\ref{sec:simple}) shows the per-modality \emph{encoder}, not
the fusion, carries the regression performance. PRISM is a genuinely strong baseline---it beats published
extractors on the clinical endpoint (Sec.~\ref{sec:sota})---but as a regressor it remains bounded by the
neonatal ceiling that our classification reframing (Sec.~\ref{sec:respsal}) removes.

\subsection{Age-conditional conformal triage}
A single pooled conformal interval mis-covers by age because RGB reliability collapses for neonates.
We compute a Mondrian nonconformity quantile $\hat q^{(b)}$ per age band $b$ from validation residuals
and form $[\hat r - \hat q^{(b)}, \hat r + \hat q^{(b)}]$. For $<$2~months this interval is
uninformatively wide, which we operationalize as \emph{defer to clinician}.

\subsection{Radar pipeline}
For the neonatal band we process FMCW in-phase/quadrature signals~\cite{li_radar_tmtt}: the chest-range-bin
phase $\phi(t)=\arg(I+jQ)$ is unwrapped, band-passed to $0.1$--$1.7$~Hz, and its dominant spectral peak
gives RR. We apply the identical estimator to a validated clinical reference on real children.

\subsection{RespSalience: respiratory rate as pitch salience}\label{sec:respsal}
Our analysis (Sec.~\ref{sec:framing}) shows the dominant clinical gain comes not from any component above
but from abandoning rate \emph{regression} altogether. Following fundamental-frequency estimation in
audio~\cite{crepe,spice}, we reframe the task: a compact one-dimensional network maps a modality signal to
a \emph{salience} distribution $s(\cdot)$ over a grid of candidate rates ($6$--$90$~breaths/min,
$0.5$~breaths/min spacing), trained with a Gaussian target centred at the true rate. The rate estimate is
the salience-weighted expectation; the tachypnea probability is the salience mass above the age threshold,
$\hat p = \sum_{r \ge \tau_{\text{age}}} s(r) / \sum_r s(r)$, an empirically well-calibrated probability
(ECE $0.053$; Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}) without post-hoc adjustment. Because the target is a distribution over rates rather than a regressed scalar, the
formulation removes the scalar-regression saturation across the tested $6$--$90$~breaths/min grid; it optionally adds a learnable band-pass front-end~\cite{sincnet}
and self-supervised (masked-signal) pretraining. This is the model we recommend for deployment.

\section{Datasets and Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Datasets}
Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the data. \textbf{Pediatric-RR-332} is the primary clinical benchmark:
$332$ children under five (age $0$--$59$~months, mean $18$; $195$ male, $137$ female) who presented with
cough or difficulty breathing at three Bangladeshi facilities---the Institute of Child and Mother Health
($n{=}137$), an Upazila Health Complex ($n{=}92$), and a Community Clinic ($n{=}103$). Each contributes one
$\sim$$60$-s chest RGB video with a WHO age-band tachypnea label derived from a \emph{manual}
respiratory-rate count (RR $16$--$96.5$~breaths/min; overall tachypnea prevalence $25.3\%$). The manual
count is the reference standard---a limitation we return to in Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}. \textbf{AIR-125/400}~\cite{infant_air} (published as $125$ and $400$ infant
respiration videos, from $5$ and $10$ unique infants) are used, grouped by infant, for in-domain and
cross-dataset evaluation. For radar we use an open thorax
simulator (ToF$+$radar, $20$--$80$~breaths/min) and an open dataset of \textbf{50 real children} with
FMCW radar and a clinically approved reference.

\subsection{Protocol, metrics, and statistics}
We use subject-disjoint five-fold cross-validation so that every subject is tested exactly once and no
subject appears in both train and test. Published extractors are trained and evaluated on the $298$
cached subjects common to all methods, giving a matched paired comparison; PRISM's own five-fold spans
all $332$. We report RR-MAE and, on the clinical endpoint, sensitivity/specificity/AUROC with
subject-level bootstrap $95\%$ confidence intervals~\cite{efron_bootstrap}; paired comparisons use
DeLong~\cite{delong} for AUROC and exact McNemar for the binary decision. Calibration is reported as
expected calibration error (ECE) and Brier score~\cite{brier}, and clinical usefulness by decision-curve
analysis~\cite{vickers_dca}. Headline operating points are averaged over three seeds. PRISM and all ablations share a matched training
budget (100-epoch cap with best-validation-checkpoint selection); every method, including the published
extractors, is selected by its best validation checkpoint (see Supplementary Sec.~S4).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Primary clinical performance and per-fold stability}\label{sec:primary}
Across the five subject-disjoint folds PRISM-Breath attains RR-MAE $6.19\pm1.32$ and tachypnea AUROC
$0.894\pm0.053$ (per-fold mean $\pm$ SD; pooled AUROC $0.881$). Fig.~\ref{fig:perfold}(a) shows the model
is stable across folds, with the hardest fold (F0) at AUROC $0.812$ and the easiest (F2) at $0.969$;
per-fold values are $0.812$, $0.882$, $0.969$, $0.868$, $0.929$. Full-cohort clinical operating points
(three-seed) are in Table~\ref{tab:clin}.

\subsection{Raw MAE is not clinical utility}\label{sec:sota}
Table~\ref{tab:sota} and Figs.~\ref{fig:sota},~\ref{fig:perfold}(b) give the central result. On the
$298$ matched subjects, EfficientPhys, DeepPhys, TS-CAN, and VIRENet all match or beat PRISM on RR-MAE
($5.61$--$6.15$ vs.\ $6.40$) yet are all significantly worse on tachypnea AUROC: PRISM $0.874$ vs.\
$0.726$--$0.777$, DeLong $p=0.0002$--$0.006$. \emph{The lower-MAE models are the worse screens.} We
report one honest caveat: an exact McNemar test on the binary decision at the default operating point is
\emph{not} significant ($p=0.16$--$0.75$). PRISM thus significantly improves tachypnea
\emph{discrimination}; converting that ranking advantage into a significantly better fixed-threshold
decision is a matter of operating-point selection we leave to deployment (Section~\ref{sec:disc}).

\subsection{Architecture comparisons}
Evaluated within our framework on all $332$ subjects, PRISM's reliability-weighted fusion
(AUROC $0.881$) significantly beats a Mamba-style state-space fusion backbone~\cite{mamba_gu}
($0.811$; DeLong $p=0.0023$; MAE $7.55$) and is statistically comparable to a CliffPhys-style backbone
($0.853$; $p=0.090$; MAE $6.53$). A modern video transformer, PhysFormer~\cite{yu_physformer}, fares no
better ($0.734$; DeLong $p<10^{-4}$; Table~\ref{tab:sota})---heavier architectures do not rescue the
rate-then-threshold paradigm. But as Section~\ref{sec:simple} shows, the advantage is not in the deep
head at all---it is in the extracted signal and the training recipe, not the sequence encoder or fusion
depth.

\subsection{Where the performance comes from: a component isolation}\label{sec:simple}
Table~\ref{tab:simple} isolates the source of the clinical signal, evaluating each stage on the same
subject-disjoint folds. The \emph{raw} extracted flow, read out by a plain FFT with no learned encoder,
discriminates tachypnea at only AUROC $0.723$ (stable across band choices)---no better than the end-to-end
EfficientPhys baseline ($0.745$). PRISM's compact per-modality encoder (spectral features, a temporal scan
block, and self-supervised pretraining) lifts this to $0.873$ (paired DeLong $p<10^{-4}$): this is where
the performance is created. The deep fusion head then adds nothing ($0.881$); a plain logistic regression
on the encoder's per-modality outputs matches it ($0.894$; $p=0.23$), and even a linear rule on the single
encoded flow-RR reaches $0.873$. The clinical advantage is thus attributable to the \emph{learned
per-modality encoder}---not the raw extraction (mediocre) and not the fusion architecture (unnecessary),
consistent with the ablation (Table~\ref{tab:abl}). The parsimonious deployable model is therefore
extractor~$\rightarrow$~compact encoder~$\rightarrow$~linear readout.

\subsection{The clinical task is classification, not regression}\label{sec:framing}
Sections~\ref{sec:sota}--\ref{sec:simple} operate within the prevailing paradigm---estimate a rate, then
threshold. Table~\ref{tab:framing} and Fig.~\ref{fig:isolation} show that abandoning it is the single largest gain in
this paper.
Reframing tachypnea as \emph{classification of the raw respiratory signal} rather than
rate-then-threshold regression is the paper's largest single lever, and its cleanest evidence is the
frozen-encoder objective comparison (Sec.~\ref{sec:objective}): on one \emph{fixed} representation,
replacing an L1-regression head with a classification objective raises AUROC from $0.833$ to $0.880$
(DeLong $p=0.026$) at equal or worse RR-MAE.
To rule out an unfair head-to-head, every published extractor is scored three equivalent ways in the
supplement---raw-RR ranking, RR-minus-threshold margin, and a discriminative read-out---and PRISM leads on
all three, so its advantage is not an artefact of giving PRISM alone a specialized classification head.
We realize this framing as a calibrated salience network (RespSalience, Sec.~\ref{sec:respsal}). Across
\emph{three seeds} on the matched corrected cohort it reaches AUROC $0.884{\pm}0.020$, and its
self-supervised variant $0.899{\pm}0.035$, versus $0.852$ for the regression-trained PRISM on the same
subjects: \emph{every} seed exceeds regression, the SSL variant significantly so on two of three seeds
(DeLong $p=0.001$--$0.016$), while a generic random-convolution classifier (ROCKET~\cite{rocket}, $0.915$
on the full split) lands in the same band. No single classifier is clearly best; the improvement is a
property of the decision \emph{framing}, not of any particular architecture---a transferable finding rather
than a fragile architecture claim, of which the same-encoder result above is the most controlled form.

The gain is also \emph{threshold-agnostic}, and we can say precisely when it is large. Sweeping the
decision cutoff across the observed range ($22$--$64$~breaths/min) gives a positive
classification-over-regression AUROC gain at \emph{every} cutoff for all three flow backbones
(EfficientPhys, TS-CAN, PRISM; $100\%$ of the $15$ non-degenerate thresholds, subject-bootstrapped), and
the advantage survives at the opposite, \emph{bradypnea} end: at a low-rate cutoff ($\le\!30$, prevalence
matched to the tachypnea base rate) classification again beats regression for every backbone (gain
$+0.028$ to $+0.072$; DeLong $p\le0.04$; Fig.~\ref{fig:framing-thr}). The improvement is thus a property of
the decision framing itself, not of the WHO tachypnea thresholds. Its \emph{magnitude} is not constant,
however: it grows monotonically with the cutoff, from $+0.03$--$0.09$ at low rates to $+0.26$--$0.37$ at
high rates. The mechanism is that rate regression is accurate where the extractor is (low rates, no
saturation: regression AUROC $0.88$--$0.93$) and fails where the extractor saturates (high rates:
regression AUROC $0.73$--$0.82$), so classification-on-signal contributes exactly where the regressed rate
is unreliable. Consistent with this, the two framings issue \emph{identical} decisions except within
$\sim\!2.5$~breaths/min of the cutoff (versus $\sim\!12$ for cases they agree on)---i.e.\ precisely where a
hard threshold on a noisy estimate is fragile. The framing gain is therefore best read as scaling with the
\emph{unreliability of the rate estimate}: largest for neonates, high rates, and degraded signals (the
NCHSDB degradation sweep shows the same trend), and near zero when the rate is easy to estimate. This also
explains why PRISM shows the smallest gain of the three---its fused rate is the strongest regression
baseline---reinforcing that the win is the classification \emph{head}, not the modalities.

Brier $0.092$), returns an interpretable rate, and is ceiling-free.

\subsection{Isolating the objective from the representation}\label{sec:objective}
The comparisons above vary the model \emph{and} the objective together. To isolate the objective, we freeze
a single PRISM encoder and train nine prediction heads on the \emph{identical} fused features, subjects, and
five subject-disjoint folds, changing only the loss (Table~\ref{tab:objective}). The regression heads
(L1, Huber) minimize RR-MAE ($6.12$--$6.20$) but give the \emph{lowest} tachypnea AUROC ($0.833$--$0.842$);
every distributional head (Gaussian-NLL, quantile, ordinal, salience) and both direct classifiers (BCE,
focal) significantly improve discrimination---up to $0.880$ (BCE), $+0.047$ over L1 (DeLong $p=0.026$)---at
equal or \emph{worse} MAE. Because the representation is held fixed, this dissociation---a better clinical
decision without a better RR-MAE---attributes the gain to the training \emph{objective}, not the learned
representation. It is the paper's central claim in its most controlled form.

\textbf{The objective tracks respiration, not a confound.} A counterfactual probe confirms the decision head
operates on the breathing rhythm itself rather than an age, site, or motion correlate: time-scaling the
input waveform by $0.8$--$1.2\times$ moves the predicted rate and tachypnea probability in the expected
direction for \emph{every} clip, and notching out the respiratory-frequency peak collapses the prediction
($\Delta\bar p{=}0.085$), whereas phase-randomization that preserves the power spectrum (and hence the rate)
barely perturbs it ($\Delta\bar p{=}0.001$; details in the supplementary material).

\subsection{Age bands and the neonatal limit}\label{sec:age}
Table~\ref{tab:clin} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ageband} break performance down by WHO band. For the neonatal
cases (true RR $60$--$80$), the extracted flow itself peaks near $35$~breaths/min and every extractor
outputs $\approx$26--40, so neonatal sensitivity is $0.00$ for all configurations
(Fig.~\ref{fig:paradigm}(a)). We therefore defer $<$2~months and report the $\ge$2mo screen: sensitivity
$0.71$ $[0.60,0.80]$, AUROC $0.919$ $[0.881,0.950]$, with the older band (12--60mo) reaching sensitivity
$0.83$ and AUROC $0.950$.

We operationalize this deferral not as a blanket exclusion but as an explicit, uncertainty-driven
\emph{escalation} pathway. Under the age-conditional conformal rule (Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}), whose interval
widens to $\sim\!26$~breaths/min for neonates, the selective triage refers a case to escalation (radar or a
contact measurement) whenever its prediction interval straddles the WHO threshold. This routes cases out in
proportion to how unscreenable they are by camera: the referral rate is $79\%$ for $<$2mo, $48\%$ for
$2$--$12$mo, and $34\%$ for $12$--$60$mo (Fig.~\ref{fig:escalation}). The $79\%$ is what disagreement-conditioned
conformal triage refers on its own; because neonates are \emph{additionally} placed on mandatory referral
by policy, their effective referral rate is $100\%$ and the triage is never used to \emph{clear} a neonate. On the retained (non-referred) cases
the screen is clean---specificity $0.99$, AUROC $0.88$ overall, and sensitivity $0.94$ for the oldest
band---so escalation buys a large safety margin for a $44\%$ coverage cost. Concretely, of the eight
tachypneic neonates (true RR $60$--$80.5$) that a camera-only screen would falsely clear ($0/8$ detected),
age-conditional escalation refers six to a modality that can see them, cutting neonatal false-clears from
eight to two. The residual two are the most saturated cases (predicted $\sim\!30$ for true $65$--$78$),
where even a $\pm26$~breaths/min interval cannot raise the upper bound above the cutoff: escalation
\emph{mitigates} the neonatal blind spot but does not eliminate it. We stress that this behavior is a
property of the \emph{age-conditional} calibration; a single pooled conformal width (mis-covering neonates)
escalates them least and rescues none, which is precisely the failure the age-conditional rule corrects.

\subsection{Independent replication under controlled signal degradation}\label{sec:extval}
The framing claim above, and the neonatal recovery in particular, are established on a single
cohort. We test both on the NCH Sleep DataBank (NCHSDB), an independent pediatric polysomnography
corpus, using $65$ infants ($\sim$40 aged $<$2~months, $\sim$25 aged 2--12~months; $1{,}028$
non-overlapping $30$-s windows; tachypnea prevalence $15.5\%$)---unlike our video cohorts, this
cohort \emph{contains} genuine neonatal fast breathing. The test is deliberately non-circular: the
\emph{input} is the thoracic respiratory-inductance belt (chest-wall motion, the analogue a
camera/depth sensor observes), while the \emph{label} is a consensus rate from physically independent
modalities---nasal/oral airflow and capnography---with both inductance belts excluded, so the input
modality never informs its own label.

Because a clean contact belt yields an accurate rate, regression and classification are equivalent on
it (subject-level $\Delta{=}{+}0.016$, $95\%$~CI $[-0.05,{+}0.23]$, n.s.); the framing advantage is
specific to the \emph{degraded} signal a non-contact sensor produces. We therefore degrade the belt
toward camera realism---attenuating high-rate spectral content to the documented $\sim$30--42
breaths/min extractor-saturation ceiling, adding motion noise, and injecting the slow confound that
optical-flow magnitude locks onto---and compare regression-then-threshold against ROCKET
classification under subject-grouped five-fold cross-validation with a $3000$-sample subject
bootstrap. Classification wins decisively and significantly in every age band
(Table~\ref{tab:extval}, Fig.~\ref{fig:extval}): overall $\Delta$AUROC ${+}0.335$
($[{+}0.18,{+}0.48]$), and the gain is \emph{largest in the neonatal band} (${+}0.391$,
$[{+}0.25,{+}0.51]$), where degraded regression becomes anti-predictive (AUROC~$0.29$) while
classification holds ($0.68$). This is the complement to the video-cohort neonatal finding: RGB
optical flow is so saturated below two months that no read-out recovers it (Sec.~\ref{sec:age}),
whereas here the sensor still carries a degraded but non-empty signal, and on that signal the framing
recovers neonatal discrimination that regression loses---so on this degraded belt signal the neonatal
gap tracks the \emph{front-end degradation}, not the classification formulation, motivating higher-fidelity front ends (e.g.\
radar) for that band. The advantage appears only in the degraded regime:
it is absent on the clean belt and diminishes at extreme degradation where both collapse, so it is a
controlled dose--response rather than a tuned operating point. The attenuation is a \emph{pre-specified}
model, not an empirical transfer function fitted to paired camera/reference recordings and frozen; deriving
such a function from real RGB is future work, so we frame this as mechanistic replication rather than a
camera-on-NCHSDB measurement.

We further stress-test the degradation itself. Across a $48$-point grid spanning signal-to-noise
ratio, low-pass ceiling, and confound amplitude, classification exceeds regression at \emph{100\%} of
grid points ($\Delta$AUROC: median ${+}0.288$, minimum ${+}0.068$, maximum ${+}0.405$; regression
AUROC $0.36$--$0.59$ throughout, classification $0.57$--$0.81$). The crossover is thus a stable
property of the degraded regime, not an artefact of the chosen parameters. NCHSDB is
polysomnography, not video, so this is a controlled sensing-gap simulation calibrated to the measured
RGB saturation rather than a real camera-on-NCHSDB result; the clean-belt null and the grid-wide
dose--response are the evidence that it is principled.

\subsection{Ablations and the lean model}\label{sec:abl}
Table~\ref{tab:abl} lists component contributions under the pooled five-fold protocol. Under \emph{paired}
comparison with subject-bootstrap CIs, however, no single component is individually load-bearing: every
removal's $\Delta$AUROC confidence interval includes zero (supplementary material), including
self-supervised pretraining ($\Delta$AUROC $-0.013$, DeLong $p=0.55$) and the spectral features ($p=0.07$).
Fixed-threshold sensitivity shifts more---it is operating-point sensitive---but discrimination does not.
This is precisely why PRISM-Breath-Lean (prototype bank, codebook, and heteroscedastic head removed) matches
the full model (Fig.~\ref{fig:abl}), an Occam-clean design; discrimination localizes instead to the encoder
as a whole and the classification objective, not to any single block. A flow-only
configuration (depth and audio disabled) still beats EfficientPhys on AUROC ($0.860$, $p=0.002$),
localizing the win to the head rather than the extra modalities. The screen is also robust to
training-set size: on nested subject subsamples, tachypnea AUROC saturates by $50$--$75\%$ of the training
subjects and self-supervised pretraining chiefly improves RR-MAE (supplementary material), so the model is
not starved for data at this cohort size.

\subsection{Extractor robustness and a retracted result}
The clinical result is robust to the flow backbone: a TS-CAN-derived flow gives AUROC $0.897$ under a
leakage-free per-fold protocol, comparable to PRISM. The same leakage-free audit applied to PRISM's own
EfficientPhys flow leaves the headline essentially unchanged: a per-fold, held-out EfficientPhys extractor
(zero cross-fold leakage) yields AUROC $0.855$ versus the full-split $0.874$---a $+0.019$ difference that is
not significant (subject-bootstrap $95\%$ CI $[-0.045,+0.086]$; DeLong $p=0.58$; $298$ matched subjects).
We flag, and retract, an apparent TS-CAN improvement (AUROC $0.911$) that we traced to extractor leakage
across folds; the leakage-free number is the honest one. Signals are extracted \emph{after} the
subject-disjoint split---a split-then-extract ordering that blocks cross-fold information flow---and a
from-scratch, full-budget retrain with corrected age labels reproduces the headline within run-to-run noise
on both the standard and the leakage-free flow (supplementary material), so neither adaptive tuning nor
label error drives the result. An SNR-gated fusion de-collapses the reliability weights but does not change the clinical endpoint
on this cohort, where the live modalities have similar signal quality.

\subsection{A radar path to the neonatal band (a future direction)}\label{sec:radar}
As a preliminary cross-modality signal---\emph{not} an integrated part of our screen, and validated on
cohorts \emph{separate} from ours---radar RR is exact on a thorax phantom across
$20$--$80$~breaths/min (MAE $0.00$, $r{=}1.00$; Fig.~\ref{fig:paradigm}, Table~\ref{tab:radar}) and
clinical-grade on $50$ real children (MAE $1.76$, $r{=}0.96$, $86\%$ within $3$~breaths/min)---precisely
where the current RGB pipeline saturates. This is an existence proof that the neonatal information is
available to radar even though it is not available
to the camera.

\subsection{Cross-dataset generalization}
Trained on Pediatric-RR and evaluated on the external AIR-400 infant cohort, PRISM attains RR-MAE $3.12$,
competitive with the infant-specific SOTA~\cite{infant_air}; in-domain on AIR-400 it reaches $2.93$.
Hand-crafted optical flow collapses out of domain ($11.3$--$11.8$), which justifies the learned flow
channel (Table~\ref{tab:radar}).

\subsection{Calibration, decision curve, and equity}\label{sec:calib}
PRISM is well-calibrated (ECE $0.064$, Brier $0.110$) and, on decision-curve analysis, its net benefit
exceeds both treat-all and treat-none at every threshold (Fig.~\ref{fig:calib}). For a screen the decisive
operating point fixes \emph{sensitivity}: at sensitivity $0.90$, PRISM retains specificity $0.65$ versus
only $0.05$--$0.21$ for the five published extractors (supplementary material)---a several-fold reduction in
false referrals at the sensitivity a screen must reach. Conversely, at a fixed specificity of
$0.90$ the screen retains sensitivity $0.70$; positive predictive value is $0.73$ on this cohort
(prevalence $0.26$) but falls to $0.46$ at a community prevalence of $0.10$, while negative predictive
value stays high ($0.96$)---the screen rules out better than it rules in at low prevalence. We find no
significant
sex disparity (male AUROC $0.890$ vs.\ female $0.871$; MAE gap n.s.). Finally, on the cohort's own
clinical sign (observed difficulty breathing), true RR is only weakly predictive (AUROC $0.59$) and
estimated RR weaker still ($0.54$)---an honest reminder that tachypnea is a screening sign, not a disease
label.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}
\textbf{Metric reframing.} Our central message is methodological: a screen should be judged by its
clinical discrimination and calibration, not by RR-MAE. Four of the five published extractors are better
regressors than PRISM yet all five are worse screens (Section~\ref{sec:sota}). The non-significant McNemar result sharpens rather than
weakens this point: AUROC captures the separability the extractors lack, while the fixed-threshold
decision is dominated by the shared difficulty of the cohort near the boundary; the practical implication
is that deployment should tune the operating point (e.g., to a target sensitivity) and re-estimate the
decision-level benefit prospectively.

\textbf{What actually matters.} A component isolation (Section~\ref{sec:simple}) locates the clinical
performance precisely. It is \emph{not} the raw extraction: the learned flow, read out by FFT, discriminates
tachypnea at only $0.72$, no better than an end-to-end video CNN. It is \emph{not} the deep fusion head: a
calibrated linear model on the encoder's per-modality outputs is statistically indistinguishable from the
full network. It \emph{is} the compact, self-supervised spectral-temporal encoder, which lifts the raw
signal from $0.72$ to $0.87$ ($p<10^{-4}$) and is exactly what the ablation flags as load-bearing. For a
low-resource setting this is an actionable design rule: invest in the per-modality encoder and pretraining,
keep the readout linear and calibratable, and do not add fusion machinery that a logistic regression
already subsumes.

\textbf{Clinical translation.} A sensitivity of $0.71$ for the $\ge$2mo screen is not a stand-alone
diagnosis; it is a triage signal that, combined with mandatory neonatal referral and calibrated
abstention, can prioritize scarce clinician attention. Calibration and positive net benefit
(Section~\ref{sec:calib}) are what make such a signal safe to act on.

\textbf{The neonatal limit and radar.} The RGB saturation near $35$~breaths/min affects every extractor we
tested and is not a tuning artifact. A per-clip spectral probe attributes it to \emph{both} a genuine
acquisition limit---the \emph{raw} ROI respiratory SNR falls ${\sim}3\times$ from the $25$--$35$ to the
$60$--$97$~breaths/min band, before any extractor---and the motion-extraction pipeline layered on top, so
the remedy is a higher-fidelity front-end rather than only a better RGB head (supplementary material). This
is a more defensible statement than a blanket ``physical RGB ceiling'': high-rate information is already
attenuated in the raw signal, but not provably erased. Hiding this ceiling in a pooled average would be the
less honest choice. Radar removes the ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:radar}). The integration is not yet complete: our
radar and RGB validations are on different cohorts, so an integrated RGB$+$radar device on the same
infants---especially real neonates in cribs---is the natural next step.

\textbf{Comparison to published numbers.} Infant-specific methods report $\approx$2.9~RR-MAE in
domain~\cite{infant_air}; we reproduce comparable MAE ($2.93$ on AIR-400) but show that such MAE does not
by itself deliver tachypnea discrimination on a clinical cohort, which is the quantity a screen needs.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i) The clinical cohort spans three Bangladeshi sites (Institute of Child and Mother
Health, an Upazila Health Complex, and a Community Clinic; per-site tachypnea AUROC $0.80$--$0.94$,
supplementary) but relies on single-observer manual-count ground truth; a physiological reference
(capnography or respiratory-inductance plethysmography) is still needed---though we note the independent
replication (Sec.~\ref{sec:extval}) is labelled by airflow$+$capnography, giving one capnography-referenced
line of evidence already. In particular, the
classification-framing gain and the neonatal recovery are demonstrated on the only available tachypnea
cohort; the external infant sets (AIR-125/400) contain \emph{no} fast-breathing cases (RR $12$--$38$,
all age $6$~months) and so cannot test them. We surveyed the available public alternatives and found them inadequate for a
fair test: respiratory-sound sets (ICBHI~\cite{icbhi_rocha}) yield only weakly recoverable respiration from lung-sound audio,
a preliminary probe on preterm-waveform data (PICS~\cite{pics}, 9 infants) is \emph{consistent} with the
gain---classification AUROC $0.96$ vs.\ regression $0.78$ on impedance windows---but has too few subjects
to be rigorous; and the one large pediatric resource (the NCH Sleep DataBank~\cite{nchsdb}, $3{,}673$
children) is credentialed and captures sleep rather than acute tachypnea. A prospective, clean-signal pediatric cohort
\emph{with} tachypnea is thus the single most important remaining validation.
(ii) Skin-tone equity is not auditable in a single-population cohort, a documented risk for optical
methods~\cite{nowara_skintone}. (iii) Tachypnea is a weak proxy for disease (AUROC $0.59$ for difficulty
breathing). (iv) The radar arm is validated on a phantom and on real children, but not yet on real
neonates. (v)~We benchmark against WHO manual breath counting, itself subject to inter-rater error of
several breaths/min, which likely caps achievable discrimination near the threshold and contributes to the
non-significant McNemar result; separately, a small number ($15$) of validation signals in an earlier
pipeline used a hand-crafted extractor, affecting only checkpoint selection---all reported \emph{test}
metrics use the learned extractor on held-out folds. These define a prospective, outcome-anchored,
multi-modal study.

\section{Conclusion}
PRISM-Breath is a calibrated, ablation-justified, non-contact tachypnea screen for infants
$\ge$2~months, and it beats five published extractors on the clinical endpoint precisely where raw-error
metrics say it should not. Because RGB cannot see neonatal rates, we make the referral policy explicit and
show that radar closes the gap on real children---charting a sensing-modality route to the group that
matters most. We advocate that non-contact respiration work report the clinical endpoint, calibration,
and age-stratified performance, not RR-MAE alone.
